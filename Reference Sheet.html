Therapeutic communication

What it is

Therapeutic communication is the specialized way nurses talk with patients to build a therapeutic (healing) relationship. It uses both words and body language, and it keeps the focus on the patient's needs and feelings, not the nurse's.

It came from nursing theorist Hildegard Peplau (Theory of Interpersonal Relations, 1950s). Use it in every patient encounter, not just with psych patients.

Why it matters in med-surg: anxious, scared or angry patients don't take in teaching, don't report symptoms and don't follow the plan. Good communication is how you get an accurate assessment and safe discharge teaching.

How to pick the answer on an exam

  • Pick the response that keeps the focus on the patient's feelings or concerns and invites them to say more (open-ended, reflecting, sharing an observation, offering self).
  • Rule out anything that is a "why" question, false reassurance ("don't worry", "everything will be fine"), advice ("if I were you…"), changing the subject, defending yourself or staff, judging or arguing.
  • If the patient is in physical danger (chest pain, can't breathe, suicidal), safety comes first. Act, then communicate.

Nontherapeutic techniques (avoid these)

Interrogating ("why" questions)Makes the patient defensive and they stop talking."Why didn't you take your medication like I told you?"Better: "Tell me what made it hard to take your medication."
False reassuranceComfort that isn't based on facts. It brushes off the patient's fear."Don't worry, everything will be fine."Better: let them voice the fear, then offer realistic hope.
Giving adviceYour personal opinion takes away the patient's own decision."If it were me, I wouldn't see that provider."Better: give facts and help them weigh their options.
Changing the subjectTells the patient their concern doesn't matter.Patient talks about chest pain; nurse asks, "What insurance do you have?"Better: stay on the patient's topic.
Automatic responsesReady-made phrases said without thought (clichés, stereotypes, empty promises)."I'll be right back" (and never comes back).Better: be specific and follow through.
Judgmental responsesApproving or disapproving puts the nurse above the patient."You have lung cancer because you smoked for 40 years."
Defensive responsesDefending yourself, staff or the hospital instead of hearing the complaint."I didn't do anything!" / "We're just short-staffed."Better: listen for the deeper concern behind the criticism.
Arguing / challengingImplies the patient is lying or wrong, and destroys trust.Patient raises their voice about the diet; nurse raises theirs too.
AttackingCriticizing, dominating or manipulating. If the patient felt attacked, apologize and rebuild."You came in here drunk and started hitting people."
Passive or aggressive responsesAvoiding the issue, or provoking a fight. Use assertive communication instead.Patient apologizes for soiling the bed again; nurse says, "Yeah, yeah, I know."
Not listening / not presentBeing distracted, like scrolling on a phone during the encounter.(Nurse charts and doesn't look up while the patient talks.)

Body language (nonverbal)

  • Keep words and body language congruent (matching). If they don't match, the listener believes the nonverbal message.
  • Eye contact reads as honest and respectful, especially with bad news. In some cultures it's disrespectful, so ask about the patient's preferences.
  • Head nodding shows acceptance. Touch can comfort (and helps with a blind patient), but respect boundaries and culture.

Checking that it worked: the feedback loop

  1. Reflect on the response. Restate what the patient said, so they know you heard them and keep talking.
  2. Compare the response to your goal. If what the patient says or does matches what you meant to get across, it worked.
  3. Revise the message if it didn't work, then check again. Keep going until the patient can repeat the instructions back.

After teaching, check learning with teach-back (patient restates it in their own words), return demonstration (patient shows you, e.g. drawing up insulin) or questions. "Do you understand?" is not enough: patients often say yes.

Then document: what you taught, how, the patient's response, materials given, any barriers, and what still needs teaching.

Interpreters

  • Use a certified medical interpreter (National Board of Certification for Medical Interpreters).
  • Not family: they may not know medical terms and can mistranslate. Not other staff: it can violate the patient's privacy.
  • Hospitals that take federal money (Medicare, Medicaid) must provide one (Title VI of the Civil Rights Act).

Barriers to communication

PhysicalNoise, alarms, no privacy (thin walls), time pressure, distance, broken equipment.Fix: close the door, silence alarms, find a private space, follow through on time.
EmotionalFear (doesn't hear you because of what's coming), pride (won't ask for help, then falls), anger.Fix: address the emotion first, then teach. De-escalate anger.
PerceptualPast experiences, beliefs, triggers (e.g. a trauma survivor who feels dominated), and the nurse's own biases and stereotypes.Fix: self-awareness; manage expectations ("you'll still have pain after knee surgery").
CulturalDifferent norms for eye contact, touch, family roles.Fix: cultural competence (understanding the patient's context) plus cultural humility (ongoing self-reflection).

Emotional intelligence (controlling and expressing emotions well) helps you get past barriers: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness and relationship management.

For sensory barriers, ask the patient how they prefer to learn (large print, Braille, audio, electronic) before teaching.

Motivational interviewing (MI)

A counseling approach for behavior change (quitting smoking, low-sodium diet, taking meds). Show empathy, point out the gap between what the patient does now and what they want, and accept that ambivalence (mixed feelings) is normal.

  1. Engaging: build the relationship with active listening and nonverbal cues.
  2. Focusing: pick a clear direction and goal together.
  3. Evoking: draw out the patient's own reasons to change.
  4. Planning: small, achievable SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Timely).

Professional communication

  • Empathy vs sympathy: sympathy is feeling sorry for someone; empathy is understanding and sharing their feelings while treating them as able to make their own decisions.
  • Assertiveness: confident and respectful. Speak up for patients who can't (unconscious, nonverbal, no advocate) until the concern is addressed.
  • Courtesy and names: introduce yourself, verify identity, and call patients what they prefer ("Please call me Tom").

Breathing & gases

Why air moves: Boyle's law

Pressure and volume of a gas move in opposite directions. The diaphragm contracts, the chest gets bigger, pressure inside the lungs drops below the air outside, and air rushes in. Breathing out at rest is passive: the chest gets smaller, pressure rises, air leaves.

A pneumothorax lets air into the pleural space, so the lung loses the pressure difference that keeps it open and collapses. That's what the chest tube fixes. In COPD, air gets trapped because the lungs can't push it out.

Partial pressures: Dalton's and Henry's laws

  • Dalton: each gas in a mix pushes on its own; the total is the sum. Room air is about 21% oxygen, so oxygen's share of the 760 mm Hg at sea level is about 159 mm Hg.
  • Henry: how much gas dissolves in a liquid (blood) depends on its partial pressure and how soluble it is. More pressure in, more dissolves.
  • Gases always move from higher to lower partial pressure. Alveolar O2 is about 104 mm Hg and blood arriving at the lungs is about 40, so oxygen pours in. CO2 is 45 in the blood and 40 in the alveoli, a small gap, but CO2 is about 20 times more soluble than O2, so it still crosses easily.
Supplemental oxygen raises the oxygen percentage you breathe, which raises alveolar PO2 and drives more oxygen into the blood. When the membrane thickens (pneumonia exudate, pulmonary edema, fibrosis), oxygen suffers first because CO2 crosses so much more easily, so low oxygen usually shows up before high CO2.

Matching air to blood (V/Q)

Ventilation (air in and out) and perfusion (blood through the lung capillaries) must match. The lung adjusts on its own: where alveolar oxygen is low, the small pulmonary arteries constrict and send blood elsewhere; where CO2 is high, the small airways widen.

Pulmonary embolism: air reaches the alveoli but no blood does (wasted ventilation, "dead space"). Pneumonia or atelectasis: blood flows past alveoli that get no air ("shunt"). Both lower the PaO2.

Surfactant and compliance

Surfactant, made by type II alveolar cells, lowers the surface tension of the water lining the alveoli. Without it, alveoli collapse when you breathe out. Compliance is how easily the lungs and chest wall stretch.

Stiff, low-compliance lungs (pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary edema, ARDS) take more work to inflate. In emphysema the lungs stretch easily but lose their recoil, so air gets trapped. Deep breathing and incentive spirometry after surgery keep alveoli open.

How oxygen rides in the blood

  • Almost all oxygen rides on hemoglobin; only a small amount is dissolved in plasma. Each hemoglobin has 4 hemes with iron, so it carries 4 O2. Each one that binds makes the next bind more easily, which gives the S-shaped curve.
  • SpO2 measures how full the hemoglobin is (saturation). PaO2 on an ABG measures the oxygen dissolved in plasma. They're linked by the oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve.
  • The top of the curve is flat, so PaO2 can fall quite a bit with little change in saturation. Below about 60 mm Hg the curve is steep: small drops in PaO2 mean big drops in saturation. That's why an SpO2 under about 90% is a warning sign.

Working muscle, fever and acidosis all unload more oxygen to tissues, which helps them. In alkalosis (for example, hyperventilation) hemoglobin holds on tighter, so tissues can get less oxygen even with a normal-looking SpO2.

How CO2 rides in the blood

  • About 70% as bicarbonate. Inside red cells, the enzyme carbonic anhydrase turns CO2 and water into carbonic acid, which splits into H+ and HCO3−. In the lungs it runs backward and CO2 is breathed out.
  • About 20% bound to hemoglobin (carbaminohemoglobin), and about 7–10% dissolved in plasma.
  • Haldane effect: hemoglobin that has dropped its oxygen picks up CO2 more easily, and oxygen-loaded hemoglobin holds less CO2.

This one equation links breathing to pH: whatever raises CO2 raises H+ (acid). Read it left to right for respiratory acidosis.

What tells you to breathe

  • The main trigger is CO2, not oxygen. Central chemoreceptors in the brainstem sense the acid that CO2 makes and speed up breathing.
  • Peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid arteries and aortic arch sense H+, and they only react to oxygen once it's quite low, about 60 mm Hg or less.
In some patients with chronic CO2 retention (severe COPD), too much oxygen can push CO2 higher. Your chapter's answer is to titrate oxygen to the ordered saturation target, never to withhold it from a patient who is hypoxemic. Opioids and sedatives blunt the CO2 response: watch respiratory rate and sedation.

pH and the bicarbonate buffer

  • pH is a log scale: each whole number is a 10-fold change in acid. Normal blood is 7.35–7.45 (about 7.4). A change of 0.4 or more can be fatal.
  • A buffer soaks up added acid or base. Blood's main buffer is carbonic acid and bicarbonate, kept at about 1 : 20 (much more bicarbonate) because metabolism keeps making acid.
  • The lungs adjust the CO2 side within minutes; the kidneys adjust the bicarbonate side over hours to days.
Hypoventilation (COPD exacerbation, oversedation) → CO2 builds → respiratory acidosis. Hyperventilation (anxiety, early asthma attack, PE) → CO2 blown off → respiratory alkalosis. In chronic COPD the kidneys hold onto bicarbonate, so the pH can be near normal with a high PaCO2 (compensation).

Osmosis and tonicity

Water crosses membranes toward the side with more dissolved particles. Red blood cells swell and can burst in a hypotonic solution, keep their shape in an isotonic one, and shrivel in a hypertonic one.

Blood is only ever hung with 0.9% sodium chloride, which is isotonic and doesn't damage red cells. The same idea (proteins pulling water) explains edema below.

Heart & vessels

Two kinds of heart cells, two kinds of electricity

Pacemaker (conducting) cellsNo stable resting point. Sodium slowly leaks in, raising the charge from about −60 mV to threshold (the "prepotential"), then the cell fires on its own. The SA node gets there fastest, about 80–100 times a minute with no nerve input, so it sets the rate.
Working (contractile) cellsRest at about −80 mV (atria) and −90 mV (ventricles). They fire only when an impulse reaches them: fast sodium rush in (depolarization) → plateau while calcium flows in → potassium flows out (repolarization).

The plateau gives a long refractory period (about 250 ms) when the cell can't fire again, so the heart fully contracts and refills instead of cramping.

These channels are the targets of the antidysrhythmic classes in Ch 28: sodium-channel blockers (class I), beta-blockers slowing the SA/AV nodes (II), potassium-channel blockers that lengthen repolarization and the QT (III, amiodarone), calcium-channel blockers slowing the AV node (IV, diltiazem). Potassium and calcium levels change the same channels, which is why abnormal K+ or Ca2+ causes dysrhythmias (high calcium shortens the QT).

The brakes and the accelerator

Sympathetic nerves (norepinephrine, epinephrine) raise heart rate and squeeze harder (positive chronotrope and inotrope). The vagus nerve (parasympathetic) releases acetylcholine, which opens potassium channels at the SA and AV nodes and slows the rate. Thyroid hormone also raises rate and force.

Vagal maneuvers (bearing down) slow some tachycardias; atropine blocks the vagus to speed a symptomatic bradycardia. Hyperthyroid patients run fast heart rates.

Cardiac output and the Frank-Starling law

Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume  ·  Stroke volume = end-diastolic volume − end-systolic volume (about 70 mL)
  • Preload = how full and stretched the ventricle is at the end of filling. Frank-Starling law: within limits, more stretch gives a stronger squeeze, so the heart pumps out what comes in.
  • Contractility = how hard it squeezes, regardless of stretch.
  • Afterload = the resistance the ventricle pushes against to open its valve and eject.
  • A very fast rate cuts filling time, so preload and stroke volume fall. The atria's own squeeze (atrial kick) adds the last 20–30% of filling.
In heart failure the ventricle is stretched past the helpful range, so more filling no longer means a stronger beat. That's why diuretics and nitrates (lower preload), ACE inhibitors and other vasodilators (lower afterload) and inotropes (raise contractility) each help. Atrial fibrillation loses the atrial kick, and a fast ventricular rate cuts filling time, so cardiac output drops.

Blood pressure, resistance and pulse pressure

  • Blood pressure depends on cardiac output and resistance. Resistance depends mostly on vessel radius, raised to the 4th power: halve the radius and resistance goes up about 16 times. Thicker blood (higher viscosity, like polycythemia) also raises it.
  • Compliance: arteries that stretch absorb each beat; stiff arteries (aging, atherosclerosis) push systolic pressure up.
  • Pulse pressure (systolic − diastolic) should be at least about 25% of the systolic. A narrow one points to a small stroke volume: heart failure, aortic stenosis, blood loss.
Small changes in arteriole size (vasoconstrictors, vasodilators, atherosclerotic narrowing) have huge effects on pressure and flow. That's why plaque that narrows an artery by half has such a big effect in PAD and coronary disease.

Capillary exchange: why edema happens

  • Capillary hydrostatic pressure (blood pressure inside the capillary) pushes fluid out into the tissues.
  • Colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure from plasma proteins, mostly albumin, pulls fluid back in, because the proteins are too big to leave.
  • Lymph vessels carry off the extra fluid that stays behind.

Getting blood back to the heart

Veins have low pressure, so return depends on one-way valves, the skeletal muscle pump (contracting calf muscles squeeze the veins) and the respiratory pump (pressure changes in the chest with each breath).

Bed rest and long flights stop the calf pump, blood pools, and the risk of DVT rises. That's why early walking, leg exercises and compression devices are part of VTE prevention, and why varicose veins form when valves fail.

Blood

Making red cells

  • When the kidneys sense low oxygen, they release erythropoietin (EPO), which tells the bone marrow to make more red cells.
  • Building them takes iron (the center of each heme), vitamin B12 and folate (needed to copy DNA so cells can divide), and copper (helps move iron).
  • Less than 20% of the iron you eat is absorbed; iron from meat is absorbed better than iron from plants.
Kidney disease → little EPO → anemia. No iron → small, pale cells (microcytic). No B12 or folate → cells can't divide properly and come out large (macrocytic). Iron-rich food teaching for iron deficiency comes straight from this.

When red cells die: bilirubin

Red cells live about 120 days. Macrophages in the spleen, liver and marrow break them down. The iron is saved (stored as ferritin or hemosiderin, carried by transferrin) and reused. The rest of the heme becomes biliverdin (green, the color of a healing bruise) and then bilirubin (yellow), which the liver puts into bile.

Fast red-cell destruction (hemolytic anemia, sickle cell crisis) or a failing liver → bilirubin builds up → jaundice (yellow skin and sclera). Sickle cells only last 10–20 days, which is why these patients are anemic and can get jaundiced.

A low neutrophil count (after chemotherapy, in leukemia) means high infection risk with few signs, because the cells that make pus and redness are missing: neutropenic precautions. High eosinophils fit allergic asthma.

How clotting works

  1. Vascular spasm: the vessel clamps down.
  2. Platelet plug: platelets stick and seal small holes.
  3. Coagulation: the extrinsic pathway (started by tissue injury) and the intrinsic pathway (started by damage inside the vessel) both lead to the common pathway: prothrombin → thrombin, which turns fibrinogen into a fibrin mesh.
  4. Fibrinolysis: later, plasmin dissolves the clot.

The liver makes most clotting factors and needs vitamin K for several of them (II, VII, IX, X). Calcium is also a clotting factor.

Warfarin blocks the vitamin K step (monitor PT/INR; antidote vitamin K). Heparin boosts antithrombin, which shuts thrombin down (monitor aPTT; antidote protamine). Alteplase (tPA) turns on plasmin to break up a clot. Liver disease means fewer factors and more bleeding.

Blood types and transfusion

  • ABO: type A cells carry A antigens, type B carry B, AB both, O neither. People already have antibodies against the antigen they lack (type A has anti-B), with no prior exposure.
  • Rh (D): about 85% of people are Rh+. An Rh− person only makes anti-Rh antibodies after exposure (a transfusion or an Rh+ pregnancy). RhoGAM prevents this in pregnancy.
  • Wrong blood → antibodies clump (agglutinate) and destroy the donor cells: a hemolytic transfusion reaction.

Infection

How germs spread

ContactDirect (touching) or indirect through fomites: contaminated objects like doorknobs, towels, equipment.
DropletLarger droplets from coughs and sneezes that travel about 1 meter (3 feet) or less: influenza and many pneumonias.
AirborneTiny particles that stay floating in the air and travel farther: tuberculosis, measles.
The route decides the precautions: droplet → a mask within close range; airborne (TB) → N95 respirator and a negative-pressure room; contact → gown and gloves and cleaning shared equipment. Hand hygiene breaks every route.

Gram stain: the first clue to the bug

Crystal violet stains every bacterium purple; iodine traps it in a thick peptidoglycan wall. Gram-positive bacteria (thick wall) stay purple. Gram-negative bacteria (thin wall plus an outer membrane) lose the purple and look pink.

  • Streptococcus pneumoniae, Gram-positive, is the most common cause of community-acquired bacterial pneumonia. It normally lives in the respiratory tract and takes over when defenses drop.
  • Klebsiella pneumoniae, Gram-negative, causes "currant jelly" sputum (blood and mucus) and is often resistant to many drugs.
The Gram stain comes back within hours and guides the first antibiotic until the culture and sensitivity results arrive. Collect sputum or blood cultures before the first antibiotic dose, or the culture may not grow.

Tuberculosis: why it's so slow and stubborn

  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis is acid-fast: its wall is full of waxy mycolic acid. That wax lets it survive inside the macrophages that swallow it and makes it grow slowly.
  • In about 90% of infected people the immune system walls it off in tubercles (latent TB). Healed lesions can calcify into a Ghon complex seen on X-ray.
  • It can reactivate later when immunity drops: older adults, alcohol use disorder, immunosuppression.
  • The Mantoux (tuberculin) skin test is a type IV (delayed, T-cell) hypersensitivity reaction, which is why it's read 48–72 hours later. A past BCG vaccine can make it falsely positive, so a chest X-ray confirms.
Slow growth means months of treatment with several drugs at once. Stopping early or skipping doses breeds multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) TB, which is why directly observed therapy and adherence teaching matter so much.

Influenza: why there's a new vaccine every year

  • An RNA virus with two surface spikes: hemagglutinin (H), which latches onto airway cells, and neuraminidase (N), which releases new viruses from the cell. That's where names like H1N1 come from.
  • Antigenic drift: small, steady changes each season, so last year's immunity and vaccine don't fully match.
  • Antigenic shift: two strains swap gene segments and make a brand-new H or N that almost nobody is immune to, which can cause a pandemic.
Oseltamivir works by blocking neuraminidase, so the virus can't spread from cell to cell, which is why it works best started early. A bacterial pneumonia can follow the flu, so a patient who improves and then worsens again needs reassessment.

Antibiotics and resistance

Antibiotics hit bacterial structures (cell wall, ribosomes) that viruses don't have, so they don't treat colds or flu. Heavy use is a selective pressure: the bacteria that happen to survive multiply. MRSA changed the protein that methicillin-type drugs target, so they no longer bind.

Teach patients to finish the full course and not to share or save antibiotics. Contact precautions for MRSA protect other patients.

Drug basics

What the body does to a drug (ADME)

AbsorptionSwallowed drugs pass through the liver before reaching the rest of the body (first-pass effect), which can inactivate a lot of the dose. IV, inhaled, sublingual and skin routes skip it. Don't put heat or ice over a skin patch: it changes absorption.
DistributionOnly drug that's free (not bound to proteins like albumin) can act. Low albumin (malnutrition, liver disease, older age) → more free drug → stronger effect and more toxicity.
MetabolismMainly the liver, using enzymes like cytochrome P450. Other drugs or foods that speed up or slow those enzymes cause drug interactions.
ExcretionMainly the kidneys. Weaker kidneys or liver (common with aging) → the drug stays longer → "start low and go slow" in older adults.

Half-life, steady state and the therapeutic window

  • Half-life = the time for half of the drug to be eliminated. Short half-life → dosed several times a day; long (over 12 hours) → once a day. Liver or kidney disease can lengthen it.
  • A drug reaches a stable level (steady state) after about 4–5 half-lives of regular dosing.
  • Therapeutic window = the range between the dose that works and the dose that's toxic. A narrow therapeutic index means those are close together, so blood levels are monitored.
  • Peak = highest level, drawn after the dose. Trough = lowest level, drawn right before the next dose.
Narrow-window drugs in Exam 2: warfarin (INR), digoxin (drug level, plus potassium), heparin (aPTT), and IV antibiotics like vancomycin or gentamicin (troughs). Time the lab draw correctly or the level is meaningless.

Mind & society

The stress response

  • Two systems: the sympathetic nervous system (adrenaline: faster heart, higher BP, faster breathing, more glucose) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis (cortisol).
  • Selye's general adaptation syndrome: alarm (fight or flight) → resistance (body adapts but stays on alert) → exhaustion (resources run out, illness follows).
Pain, fear and anxiety raise heart rate and BP, which raises the heart's oxygen demand. That's why controlling pain and anxiety is part of treating MI, angina and HF, not a comfort extra. Cortisol also raises blood sugar and slows healing and immunity.

Emotions and the heart and lungs

  • Anger and hostility, the harmful part of the "Type A" pattern, are long-term risk factors for heart disease and for worse outcomes in people who already have it.
  • Depression is linked to developing heart disease and dying from it.
  • In some people with asthma, stress and strong emotions can trigger attacks.
  • A panic attack looks like a heart attack: pounding heart, trouble breathing, sweating, dizziness, fear of dying.
Screen heart-failure and post-MI patients for depression, and teach stress management for hypertension and CAD. Never assume chest pain or shortness of breath is "just anxiety": rule out MI and PE first.

Sleep apnea: two kinds

Obstructive: the airway physically closes during sleep. Central: the brain stops sending the signal to breathe. Pauses last 10–20 seconds or more and cause brief wake-ups the person often doesn't notice, but they're exhausted during the day; drowsy driving accidents are common.

Ch 23's OSA content is the obstructive kind. CPAP keeps the airway open, but it can't fix central apnea. Ask about daytime sleepiness, and know that sedatives and opioids make apnea worse after surgery.

Social factors in health

  • Health differs by race, ethnicity, income and education, and these overlap. Example: in 2018, White men's life expectancy was 78.8 years and Black men's 74.7. Differences in care remain even after accounting for insurance.
  • Education matters: once smoking was linked to lung cancer and poor diet and inactivity to heart disease, people with more education and money changed habits faster, and the disease gap widened.
  • Stigma (Goffman): illnesses society judges (smoking-related lung disease, TB) can push people away from care and change the care they receive.
Ask whether the patient can afford their heart-failure medications or inhalers, get to follow-up, and has stable housing; crowded or unstable housing matters for TB. Avoid judgmental responses (see therapeutic communication above): a smoker with COPD still needs help, not blame.
