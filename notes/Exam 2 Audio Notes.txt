CHAPTER 22: ASSESSMENT OF THE RESPIRATORY SYSTEM

On the exam: 5 questions — health promotion and maintenance, calculating pack-years, age-related changes of the respiratory system.
This chapter is the toolkit every later respiratory chapter uses. You are answering one question in four ways — inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation — and the question is whether air is reaching alveoli that can actually exchange it. Learn what each finding means mechanically and you will never memorize a list of adventitious sounds again.

Connection Map. How this chapter fits together.

1, Normal physiology. Air moves in (ventilation), crosses a one-cell membrane (diffusion), and binds hemoglobin; CO₂ leaves the same way.
2, What fails. Any step can fail: blocked airway, stiff or flooded alveoli, weak muscles, or depressed drive.
3, What the nurse sees. Tachypnea, accessory muscles, tripoding, restlessness, adventitious sounds, falling SpO₂.
4, What proves it. SpO₂ (oxygenation), ETCO₂ and ABG PaCO₂ (ventilation), CXR, PFTs, bronchoscopy, thoracentesis.
5, What targets it. Oxygen, upright position, airway clearance — and fixing whichever step failed.
6, If it worsens. Fatigue leads to hypoventilation leads to rising CO₂ leads to acidosis leads to respiratory failure.
7, Emergency trigger. Silent chest, stridor, new confusion, SpO₂ less than 90%, RR falling with drowsiness, tracheal deviation.
Cause and effect. Opioid or sedative leads to respiratory center depressed leads to slow, shallow breathing leads to CO₂ rises while SpO₂ looks fine on oxygen leads to monitor RR, sedation and ETCO₂, not SpO₂ alone
Fluid in alveoli leads to fine crackles that do not clear with cough leads to longer diffusion path leads to SpO₂ falls leads to oxygen plus treating the fluid cause
Rhonchi vs fine crackles
Rhonchi = secretions in big airways, often clear with cough. Fine crackles = alveoli popping open against fluid, do not clear.
Dead space vs shunt
Dead space (PE) = air without blood. Shunt (pneumonia) = blood without air — responds poorly to oxygen alone.
Why the drug makes sense
Amiodarone/bleomycin leads to lung injury; ACE inhibitors leads to bradykinin leads to dry cough; nonselective beta blockers leads to beta2 blocked leads to bronchospasm.
Connections to other chapters
Every later chapter uses this toolkit: ch 24 obstruction, ch 25 consolidation, ch 29 pulmonary edema. Anemia (ch 34) lowers oxygen content even with normal SpO₂.
Monitor
Stable SpO₂ at the client’s baseline, clear speech, unchanged sounds.
Act now
New confusion, stridor, silent chest, sudden dyspnea after a procedure, SpO₂ less than 90% not responding.

Down to the anatomy and physiology.

Upper airway — nose and sinuses (warm, humidify, filter), pharynx, larynx with the epiglottis and vocal cords. This is where obstruction is mechanical and fast, and where the epiglottis protects the lungs from everything you swallow.
Lower airway — trachea, right and left mainstem bronchi (the right is more vertical, which is why aspiration and a dislodged tube go right), bronchioles with smooth muscle that constricts in asthma, and alveoli lined with surfactant.
Alveolar-capillary membrane — one cell thick on each side. Diffusion happens down a pressure gradient, so anything that thickens the membrane (fluid, fibrosis, pus) or removes surface area (collapse, resection, emphysema) reduces it.
Ventilation versus perfusion (V/Q) — air without blood is dead space (pulmonary embolism); blood without air is a shunt (pneumonia, atelectasis, pulmonary edema). Both cause hypoxemia; only one improves much with oxygen alone.
Control of breathing — central chemoreceptors respond to CO₂ and pH (the normal drive); peripheral chemoreceptors respond to low O₂. This is the physiology behind the caution about high-flow oxygen in chronic CO₂ retainers.
Aging changes — alveoli lose elasticity, the chest wall stiffens, respiratory muscle strength falls, cough and cilia weaken, and PaO₂ falls with age. Same pneumonia, fewer symptoms, less reserve.
Why
Every respiratory finding you will be asked about is one of four mechanical problems: the airway is narrowed, the alveoli are collapsed, the membrane is thickened, or the blood is not arriving. Wheeze = narrowed. Crackles = fluid or reopening alveoli. Diminished = air not getting there. Absent with tracheal deviation = a space filled by air or blood pushing the lung away. Naming the mechanism gives you both the priority and the intervention.

Inhalation injury and smoking cessation.

The single biggest modifiable factor — exposure to inhalation irritants, above all cigarette smoke, is the most common cause of chronic respiratory problems and physical limitation. The damaging compounds are tar, nicotine, and carbon monoxide.
Quantify it — pack-years = packs per day × years smoked. Ask separately about secondhand exposure (same environment) and thirdhand (smoke clinging to hair, clothing, and surfaces).
Catch the social smoker — ask "do you ever smoke in social situations?" They do not identify as smokers, yet intermittent smoking still damages and still leads to addiction.
Gauge dependence — do you smoke as soon as you wake? wake at night to smoke? find it hard not to smoke where it is prohibited? smoke when ill? More yes answers, greater dependence.
The 5 As — Ask about use, Advise to quit, Assess willingness, Assist with the attempt, Arrange follow-up. It is a brief intervention, under three minutes, and it is a nursing responsibility at every encounter.
Nicotine replacement — patches, gum, and lozenges over the counter; nasal spray and inhaler by prescription. Never smoke while using NRT — the combined nicotine load raises the risk of stroke and heart attack.
Drugs — bupropion reduces cravings, withdrawal, and associated depression but carries a black box warning for suicidal thoughts and behavior: teach the patient and family to report any change in thinking or behavior immediately. Varenicline blocks nicotine receptors so smoking is less rewarding.
Vaping and hookah — hookah exposure is as great as or greater than cigarettes despite the widespread belief it is safer. EVALI (e-cigarette or vaping product use–associated lung injury) has caused serious injury in people vaping under two years, with higher risk from THC-containing products.
Occupational and environmental screening — I PREPARE — Investigate exposures, Present work, Residence, Environment, Past work, Activities and hobbies, Resources and referrals, Educate. Higher-risk trades include coal miners, stone masons, welders, woodworkers, potters, bakers, printers, and grain and foundry workers.
Why
Exposure history is a physical assessment finding, not social small talk — it changes the differential before you touch the patient. And the reason the 5 As are compressed into three minutes is that willingness to quit is not a precondition for the conversation: advice given briefly and repeatedly at every contact is what moves patients between stages, which is why "the patient isn't interested" is not a reason to skip it.

Questions to ask this patient.

"How far can you walk before you have to stop — and has that changed?"
"How many pillows do you sleep on? Do you wake up short of breath?"
"Tell me about the cough. Is anything coming up? What color, how much?"
"Did you ever smoke? How many packs a day, and for how many years?" — then compute pack-years.
"What do you do, or did you do, for work? Any dust, chemicals, asbestos, or fumes?"
"Which inhalers do you have at home? Show me how you use them."
"What makes it worse — cold air, activity, lying down, mornings?"
Why
Dyspnea has no lab value — the only measure is what the patient can no longer do, which is why the useful question is about blocks and stairs rather than "are you short of breath." "Show me how you use it" is also the whole inhaler item: most patients who are "adherent" are using the device wrong, and demonstration is the only assessment that detects it.

History.

Smoking in pack-years, occupational and environmental exposure using I PREPARE, allergies, childhood and family lung disease, travel and geographic history, immunizations, and current drugs — including the ones that damage lungs (bleomycin, amiodarone) and the ones that cause cough (ACE inhibitors, ARBs). Then the symptom story: cough and its timing, sputum volume in teaspoons or cups and its color, hemoptysis, chest pain, and dyspnea graded by what the patient can no longer do — blocks walked, stairs climbed, pillows needed.


CHAPTER 24: NONINFECTIOUS LOWER RESPIRATORY CONDITIONS

On the exam: 8 questions — asthma and COPD.
Below the larynx the airway stops being one lumen and becomes a tree — two mainstem bronchi, five secondary bronchi, thousands of bronchioles, then alveolar ducts and alveoli. Nothing here closes in minutes, so the chapter is not about speed; it is about chronic, progressive loss. Every disease in it narrows the tube (asthma, chronic bronchitis), destroys the elastic sac (emphysema), thickens the membrane (fibrosis), plugs it with thick mucus (cystic fibrosis), squeezes the vessel (pulmonary arterial hypertension), or fills the space with tumor (lung cancer). Name which of those six is happening and the assessment findings, the drug class, and the nursing priority all follow.

Connection Map. How this chapter fits together.

1, Normal physiology. Millions of small airways and alveoli exchange gas across a thin, elastic membrane.
2, What fails. Airways narrow (asthma, COPD), alveoli are destroyed (emphysema), secretions thicken (CF), vessels constrict (PAH), or the membrane scars (IPF).
3, What the nurse sees. Wheeze, prolonged exhalation, barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing, clubbing, JVD in right heart strain.
4, What proves it. PFTs (FEV₁/FVC; ≥12% bronchodilator response = asthma), ABGs, sweat chloride, right heart catheterization, HRCT.
5, What targets it. SABA opens airways now; ICS calms inflammation over time; oxygen to target; airway clearance; PAH vasodilators.
6, If it worsens. Air trapping leads to fatigue leads to CO₂ retention; chronic hypoxia leads to cor pulmonale.
7, Emergency trigger. Silent chest, drowsiness with rising PaCO₂, chest tube drainage greater than 100 mL/hr, tracheal deviation, interrupted epoprostenol.
Cause and effect. Airway narrowing leads to wheeze and prolonged exhalation leads to air trapping leads to rising work of breathing leads to fatigue and rising CO₂ leads to silent chest is an emergency
Beta2 agonist leads to bronchial smooth muscle relaxes leads to airways widen leads to wheeze improves leads to tremor and tachycardia because beta receptors outside the lung are stimulated too
Asthma vs COPD
Asthma is reversible and episodic; COPD is progressive and poorly reversible.
SABA vs ICS
SABA = rescue in minutes. ICS = controller over weeks; rinse mouth to prevent thrush.
Why the drug makes sense
ICS leads to local immune suppression leads to oral thrush. LABA alone in asthma leads to symptoms masked while inflammation worsens leads to must pair with ICS. Epoprostenol half-life minutes leads to interruption = rebound PAH.
Connections to other chapters
Ch 22 PFTs and ABGs; ch 29 right-sided failure (cor pulmonale); ch 34 polycythemia from chronic hypoxia.
Monitor
SpO₂ at ordered target (often 88–92% in CO₂ retainers), stable tidaling in the water seal.
Act now
Silent chest, PaCO₂ rising with confusion, continuous bubbling with new distress, facial swelling (SVC syndrome).

Down to the anatomy and physiology.

The tree — trachea leads to two mainstem bronchi leads to five secondary bronchi leads to thousands of bronchi and bronchioles leads to alveolar ducts leads to alveoli, where gas exchange happens. Air must flow through the entire tubular system for oxygen to reach the exchanging surface, so a problem anywhere along it impairs both gas exchange and systemic perfusion.
Elastic recoil is what empties the lung. Exhalation is passive: stretched elastin pulls the alveoli back down. Destroy elastin and the lung inflates but will not deflate — air is trapped, the alveoli become large flabby bullae, and surface area for exchange falls.
Proteases versus elastin — proteases normally destroy inhaled particulates. Cigarette smoke drives protease synthesis far above normal, and the excess breaks down elastin itself. Alpha₁-antitrypsin is the brake on proteases; inherited AAT deficiency removes the brake, which is why emphysema in a non-smoker or before age 40 points to genetics.
The flattened diaphragm — a hyperinflated lung pushes the diaphragm down and flat, and a flat muscle cannot generate the same pull. The patient recruits neck, chest wall, and abdominal accessory muscles, which burn more oxygen for less air. Inhalation starts before exhalation finishes, producing the uncoordinated pattern and the sensation of air hunger.
The pressure chain to the heart — trapped air, collapsed airways, and stiff alveolar walls raise lung tissue pressure and narrow the pulmonary vessels. The right ventricle, built thin for a low-pressure circuit, must generate high pressures. It hypertrophies and then fails: cor pulmonale. Normal pulmonary pressure is 15–18 mm Hg; hypertension begins above 25.
Why CO₂ retention is the late sign — CO₂ diffuses far more easily than oxygen, so hypoxemia comes first and the patient compensates by raising the rate. ABGs can look near-normal until disease is advanced. Then CO₂ is produced faster than it can be blown off: chronic hypercapnia with respiratory acidosis, and the kidney retains bicarbonate as compensation.
Why
Lower airway = millions of parallel paths, so losing some is survivable and deterioration is measured in months and years rather than minutes. That single structural fact inverts the nursing emphasis from chapter 23: here the trend matters more than any single finding, the patient's own baseline is the reference value, and the work of breathing — not the saturation number — is what tells you whether the disease is winning.

Asthma — the full picture.

Definition — a chronic disease in which reversible acute airway obstruction occurs intermittently, reducing airflow. Two mechanisms run together: inflammation narrows the lumen from the inside, and airway hyperresponsiveness constricts bronchial smooth muscle from the outside. Most adults have both — which is why two drug classes are needed. More than 3500 deaths a year in the United States; over 20 million adults affected, and about half also had it as children.
Two inflammatory types — eosinophilic asthma (about 50% of cases) is driven by IL-5; neutrophilic asthma is driven by IL-17. Symptoms are identical; the long-term effect on the epithelium differs, and some drugs are specific to one type — which is why the monoclonals are targeted by mediator.
Triggers and history — intermittent dyspnea, chest tightness, cough, wheeze, and increased mucus. Ask whether symptoms are continuous, seasonal, activity- or work-related, or worse at night; some patients have symptoms for 4–8 weeks after a cold. Atopic patients usually have other allergies. Wheezing in a non-smoker is important diagnostically.
Control level (GINA) — four questions: daytime symptoms more than twice weekly, night waking with symptoms, reliever needed more than twice weekly, activity limited by symptoms. None = controlled, 1–2 = partly controlled, 3–4 = uncontrolled.
During an attack — audible wheeze, louder on exhalation at first, prolonged exhalation, accessory muscle use with retractions at the sternum, suprasternal notch and between the ribs, inability to speak more than a few words between breaths. Long-standing severe disease gives a barrel chest: the normal AP:lateral ratio of 1:1.5 becomes 1:1 from chronic air trapping, with a flattened diaphragm and widened rib spaces.
Labs — PaO₂ falls; PaCO₂ falls early (blowing it off), then rises late with end-tidal CO₂ as retention begins. Allergic asthma shows raised serum eosinophils and IgE; sputum may contain eosinophils and Curschmann spirals (mucous plugs with shed epithelial cells).
PFTs are the diagnosis — FVC, FEV₁ and peak expiratory flow. Asthma is diagnosed when values increase by 12% or more after a bronchodilator — reversibility is the whole point. Responsiveness can be provoked with inhaled methacholine, which induces bronchospasm in susceptible adults.
Reliever versus control — SABA (albuterol, levalbuterol, terbutaline) stops an attack and premedicates before trigger activity; always carry it and check it still holds doses. LABA (salmeterol, formoterol) prevents but cannot stop an attack, needs days to build, and carries an FDA black box warning: never as sole therapy. Cholinergic antagonists / LAMAs (ipratropium short-acting, tiotropium once daily) bronchodilate and dry secretions. Methylxanthines (theophylline) are a last resort — therapeutic dose sits close to the toxic one, so blood levels are monitored.
Antiinflammatories are control drugs only — inhaled corticosteroids are the most effective (fluticasone, budesonide, mometasone); systemic steroids are short-term for moderate disease and daily only in severe. Cromolyn blocks mast cell membranes; nedocromil inhibits mediator release. Leukotriene modifiers: montelukast and zafirlukast block the receptor, zileuton blocks synthesis. Monoclonals — omalizumab (anti-IgE), dupilumab (IL-4 receptor), benralizumab/mepolizumab/reslizumab (IL-5) — and none are for an acute attack.
Inhaler technique is examinable — MDI: shake 5 seconds, tongue under the mouthpiece, inhale slowly and deeply, hold 5–10 seconds; spacer if timing is hard. DPI: exhale away from the device, inhale quickly and deeply over 2–3 seconds, hold 4–10 seconds, and never wash it with water. SMI: prime three times before first use and again after 3 days unused, don't cover the side vents, hold 10 seconds.
Action plan and self-management — the plan carries the daily controller schedule, reliever directions, personal control questions, how to step the controller up, when to call, and emergency actions. Add a symptom diary and peak-flow self-monitoring during exacerbations, after a treatment change, and whenever symptoms worsen. Exercise is recommended; adjust the routine or premedicate with a SABA rather than stopping.
Status asthmaticus — a severe life-threatening episode that intensifies once it begins and does not respond to usual therapy: extreme labored breathing, wheezing, accessory muscle use, distended neck veins. IV fluids, systemic bronchodilators, steroids, epinephrine and oxygen immediately; prepare for emergency intubation. Sudden absence of wheezing with a low saturation means complete obstruction and calls for a tracheotomy. Untreated it ends in pneumothorax and cardiac or respiratory arrest.
Why
Every asthma drug error on an exam comes from one confusion: bronchodilators open the tube, antiinflammatories calm the lining, and neither does the other's job. That is why a patient with both bronchospasm and inflammation needs two classes, why a steroid inhaler is useless in an attack, and why a LABA alone kills — it masks worsening inflammation while doing nothing about it. And notice the silent chest: a wheeze requires moving air, so its disappearance is deterioration, not improvement.

COPD — the gas exchange concept exemplar.

What it is — a collection of lower airway disorders that interfere with airflow and gas exchange, comprising emphysema and chronic bronchitis. They are separate pathologies and most patients have both. Almost 15.7 million Americans diagnosed; roughly 3 million deaths a year worldwide.
Emphysema — the sac — destruction of elastic tissue reduces recoil, so the lung hyperinflates. Alveoli are destroyed or become large and flabby; air is trapped; the diaphragm flattens; accessory muscles take over. Primary emphysema is AAT deficiency (suspect it in non-smokers or onset before 40); secondary emphysema is mainly cigarette smoke, plus pollution, occupational exposure, and childhood respiratory infection. Late disease: CO₂ retention with chronic respiratory acidosis and a low PaO₂, and a decreased diffusion capacity because the surface area is gone.
Chronic bronchitis — the tube — inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles with increased mucus and a chronic productive cough at least 3 months a year for 2 consecutive years. Irritants trigger inflammation, vasodilation, mucosal edema, congestion and bronchospasm; mucus glands grow in number and size; walls thicken. Mucus is a breeding ground, so chronic infection follows. Diffusion capacity in bronchitis alone is usually normal.
Risk — smoking is the greatest risk factor, and a 20 pack-year history or longer usually already shows PFT changes. Ask about e-cigarettes and vaping too. Asthma is itself a risk factor: COPD incidence is 12 times greater in adults with asthma, and the overlap is named asthma-COPD overlap syndrome. Seen more often in women and in adults 65 and over.
Complications — hypoxemia and acidosis, respiratory infection, cardiac failure (especially cor pulmonale), dysrhythmias (from hypoxemia, drugs, acidosis), and respiratory failure. Cor pulmonale key features: hypoxia and hypoxemia, increasing dyspnea, fatigue, an enlarged tender liver, warm cyanotic hands and feet with bounding pulses, cyanotic lips, distended neck veins, right ventricular hypertrophy, visible pulsations below the sternum, nausea or anorexia, dependent edema, metabolic and respiratory acidosis, pulmonary hypertension.
What the patient looks like — thin, muscle mass lost in the extremities but neck muscles enlarged, slow-moving, slightly stooped, sitting forward with arms braced in the orthopneic (tripod) position. Rapid shallow breathing, jerky uncoordinated respiratory movement, abdominal wall sucked in on inspiration; 40–50 breaths/min in an exacerbation demands immediate attention. Limited diaphragmatic excursion, decreased fremitus, hyperresonance on percussion, reduced breath sounds, wheezes on inspiration and expiration but crackles usually absent. A silent chest means serious obstruction or pneumothorax. Chronic bronchitis adds a dusky cyanotic appearance, copious sputum, delayed capillary refill and finger clubbing.
GOLD classification — by FEV₁ % predicted, 1 Mild ≥80%, 2 Moderate ≤50 to less than 80%, 3 Severe ≤30 to less than 50%, 4 Very severe less than 30%. PFTs are run before and after a bronchodilator; residual volume is the most affected volume, and the FEV₁/FVC ratio falls as disease progresses. The CAT (eight items, 0–5 each, score 0–40) adds an A–D symptom designation, because a GOLD 4 patient can be an A (low exacerbation risk) and a GOLD 1 patient a D (high risk).
Other labs — ABGs then oximetry for trend; polycythemia (raised Hgb/Hct) from chronic hypoxia; electrolytes, because low phosphate, potassium, calcium and magnesium reduce muscle strength; WBC and sputum culture with infection; serum AAT if there is a family history. Saturation often runs well below 90%, and patients usually know their own usual number — a change from their baseline is what requires attention.
Priority problems — 1. decreased gas exchange; 2. weight loss from dyspnea-limited intake; 3. fatigue from an oxygen supply-demand imbalance; 4. potential for pneumonia and other respiratory infections.
Interventions — airway maintenance first — assess rate, rhythm, depth and accessory muscle use before any intervention, and look for a contributing infection. Same drug classes as asthma, weighted to long-acting agents and combinations (fluticasone/vilanterol; fluticasone/umeclidinium/vilanterol), plus mucolytics (nebulised saline or acetylcysteine, oral guaifenesin). Stepped therapy: up with exacerbations, down when stable. Have the patient demonstrate inhaler technique at every visit. Assess the hospitalised COPD patient at least every 2 hours even when admitted for something else. A sudden sharp CO₂ rise or severe PaO₂ fall means escalate — NIV is preferred over intubation in stable very severe COPD with daytime hypercapnia.
The techniques — diaphragmatic breathing (supine, knees bent, hands or a book on the abdomen for resistance, chest still) and pursed-lip breathing (in through the nose, out slowly through pursed lips, exhalation at least twice as long as inhalation, never holding the breath; inhale before an activity and exhale during it). Teach both when the patient is not dyspneic. Upright with the head elevated and arms supported; out of bed to a chair for 1-hour periods two to three times a day. Controlled coughing: feet flat, shoulders inward, head slightly down, pillow hugged; after three to five breaths in through the nose and out through pursed lips, take a deeper breath and cough two or three mini-coughs on one breath, then repeat the whole sequence at least twice. Cough on arising, before meals and before bed.
Oxygen, exercise, hydration, nutrition — 2–4 L/min by cannula or up to 40% by Venturi mask, titrated slowly to an SpO₂ of 88–92% with frequent ABGs, knowing oxygen can worsen CO₂ retention — and treating hypoxemia still comes first. No open flames. Pulmonary rehabilitation: walk at a self-paced rate to symptom limit, rest, continue to 20 minutes of actual walking, at least two or three times weekly. Fluids at least 2 L/day unless contraindicated. Nutrition: body weight within 10% of ideal, four to six small meals, biggest meal when most rested, bronchodilator 30 minutes before eating, high-calorie high-protein food that is easy to chew, avoiding gas-forming and dry foods and caffeine, no fluids before or during meals if satiety is early; monitor weight and prealbumin with a dietitian.
Energy conservation — never rush morning activities; divide each task, pace with rest periods, keep items within reach, sit at a table leaning on the elbows, avoid working with the arms raised (the accessory muscles are busy stabilising the shoulders instead of breathing), don't talk while walking, and never hold the breath during effort.
Surgery — lung volume reduction surgery removes hyperinflated tissue so the remaining lung works better: candidates are under 75, smoke-free for at least 6 months, able to follow an exercise plan, with end-stage emphysema, minimal bronchitis and stable cardiac function; usually bilateral by VATS. After surgery, incentive spirometry 10 times per hour while awake, chest physiotherapy from day one, hourly pulmonary assessment. Transplantation is the treatment of choice when nothing else remains.
Transition to home — an individualised COPD action plan in green / yellow / red zones, given before discharge with follow-up appointments booked, because readmission is common and non-adherence is usually about cost, misunderstanding and transport rather than willingness. The red zone — severe dyspnea at rest, no activity possible, fever or shaking chills, confusion or drowsiness, chest pain, coughing up blood — means call 911, and the plan tells paramedics the patient is a CO₂ retainer and to keep SpO₂ at 88–92%.
Why
The 88–92% target looks like undertreatment and is not. In a chronic retainer the drive to breathe has shifted to hypoxemia, so flooding the patient with oxygen removes the stimulus and lets CO₂ climb — but withholding oxygen from a hypoxic patient kills faster than hypercapnia does. So the rule is give oxygen, titrate it slowly, and watch the CO₂, never "give less because of the COPD." Pursed-lip breathing works on the same physiology in reverse: the mild resistance raises airway pressure and holds the collapsing bronchioles open long enough to empty the trapped air.

Cystic fibrosis in the adult.

One defect, every organ — an autosomal recessive disease of the CFTR gene: blocked chloride transport across cell membranes produces mucus with little water, so it is thick and sticky. It plugs airways in the lung and glandular tissue in the pancreas, liver, salivary glands and testes. Over 2000 mutations are known, about 25 account for most patients, and the mutation explains why severity differs so much. Nearly half of US patients with CF are adults; average life expectancy is about 46 years and respiratory failure is the main cause of death.
Diagnosis — sweat chloride 60 mmol/L or more is positive; 30–59 means possible, needing more testing. Genetic testing names the mutation. Usually diagnosed by age 2, but some adults are diagnosed late.
Pulmonary picture — chest congestion, limited exercise tolerance, cough, sputum, accessory muscle use, falling FVC and FEV₁, chest x-ray infiltrate with increased AP diameter. Constant mucus means chronic infection, chronic bronchitis and bronchiectasis, with lung abscesses common; complications are pneumothorax, arterial erosion with hemorrhage, and respiratory failure.
Non-pulmonary — abdominal distention, GERD, rectal prolapse, foul-smelling stools and steatorrhea; malnutrition with fat-soluble vitamin (A, D, E, K) deficiency; pancreatic insufficiency, male sterility, cirrhosis, osteoporosis, and diabetes in more than 50% of adults with CF.
Exacerbation is defined — increased congestion, reduced activity tolerance, new or increased crackles, and a 10% decrease in FEV₁, with more sputum (bloody or purulent), more cough, appetite and weight loss, fatigue, falling SpO₂ and retractions. Treat with intensified airway clearance four times a day, bronchodilators and mucolytics, steroids, oxygen by SpO₂, and a 14- to 21-day antibiotic course guided by sputum — IV if oral fails or the exacerbation is severe. Avoid mechanical ventilation wherever possible; BiPAP may be daily therapy in advanced disease.
Airway clearance — positive expiratory pressure, active cycle of breathing, individualised exercise, and daily chest physiotherapy with postural drainage. The HFCWO vest compresses and releases the chest wall up to 25 times per second, creating mini-coughs that dislodge and thin mucus.
The two organisms — Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the most common. Burkholderia cepacia is the dangerous one: often antibiotic-resistant and spread by casual contact between people with CF, so infected and non-infected patients are separated on units and seen on different clinic days, equipment and rooms get CF-approved cleaning, and patients are taught not to shake hands routinely and to wash hands meticulously, since surfaces and tissues also carry it. Treatment adds co-trimoxazole.
Modulators — ivacaftor (a potentiator) holds the chloride channel open longer for responsive mutations; ivacaftor/lumacaftor is a corrector for the F508del mutation, the most common one; the FDA-approved triple combination ivacaftor + tezacaftor + elexacaftor now helps up to 90% of patients. Recurrent hemoptysis may need arterial embolisation, sometimes repeatedly. Transplantation relieves lung disease only — the pancreatic and other organ problems continue, and lifelong antirejection therapy raises infection risk.
Why
Every feature of CF is downstream of water. Chloride cannot move, so water does not follow it into the secretions, so every secretion in the body is too thick to flow: mucus plugs the bronchiole, enzymes never leave the pancreas, bile thickens in the liver. That is why the therapy is mechanical (vest, drainage, mucolytics) rather than merely pharmacologic, and why the newest drugs are aimed at the channel itself instead of the symptoms.

Pulmonary arterial hypertension and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.

PAH — the numbers — pulmonary hypertension is a chronic pulmonary vascular pressure above 25 mm Hg when normal is 15–18. The thin-walled right ventricle must overcome it, and over time that is cor pulmonale, which can be fatal. Secondary pulmonary hypertension follows COPD or fibrosis — controlling the cause delays cor pulmonale. Primary (idiopathic) PAH is vascular and lung tissue growth change for unknown reasons, diagnosed only in the absence of other lung disease.
Who and how fast — mostly women aged 30–60, clustering in families; the familial form is autosomal dominant, and 15–20% carry a BMPR2 mutation whose over-activation thickens pulmonary arterial smooth muscle. Appetite-suppressant exposure (fenfluramine/phentermine, dexfenfluramine) raises risk. Without treatment death usually occurs within 2 years, most often from heart failure — and it is often diagnosed late, after the heart and lungs are already damaged. Teach adults, especially women, with a first-degree relative to have regular checks.
Presentation and diagnosis — the earliest symptoms are dyspnea and fatigue in an otherwise healthy adult, sometimes angina-like chest pain. Diagnosis is right-sided heart catheterisation showing elevated pulmonary pressures; V/Q scanning, PFTs with reduced volumes and diffusion, and CT support it. Class I no activity limitation leads to II no symptoms at rest, symptoms with mild-moderate activity leads to III symptoms with less than ordinary activity leads to IV dyspnea and fatigue at rest with signs of right heart failure.
Drugs and the teaching that goes with them — endothelin receptor antagonists (bosentan, ambrisentan, macitentan): take with a full glass of water, do not break, chew or crush; all cause some hypotension, carry liver toxicity (teach jaundice, nausea, dark urine, right upper quadrant pain, and no alcohol), and are contraindicated in pregnancy — two reliable methods of contraception and monthly pregnancy testing. Prostacyclin agonists: IV epoprostenol by continuous central infusion must never be stopped abruptly (rebound pulmonary hypertension); treprostinil can be subcutaneous, IV, inhaled or oral. Phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil) improve exercise capacity and cause general and postural hypotension. Oxygen to keep saturation above 90%, diuretics with daily weights. Refer to a specialised centre — PAH-specific therapy can harm the wrong patient. Surgery is lung transplantation, or heart-lung transplantation once cor pulmonale is present.
IPF — excessive healing — a restrictive disease of unknown cause, most often men aged 50–70, with smoking and inhalation irritant exposure as risks. Inflammation that should stop continues past normal healing, and fibrosis and scarring thicken alveolar tissue until gas exchange fails. Death usually occurs within 3–5 years of diagnosis.
IPF findings and care — slow onset with mild exertional dyspnea, decreased FVC, and a "honeycomb" pattern on high-resolution CT; later, rapid shallow breathing and hypoxemia severe enough to resist high-flow oxygen. Nintedanib (tyrosine kinase inhibitor) and pirfenidone (antifibrotic) slow progression and are started early; corticosteroids, N-acetylcysteine and immunosuppressants are also used. Transplantation is the only cure and rarely available. Nursing focus: prevent respiratory infection, teach continuous home oxygen, energy conservation and pacing for profound fatigue, and in late disease oral, parenteral or nebulised morphine for the sensation of dyspnea, with hospice when survival is expected to be under 6 months.
Why
These two diseases are the chapter's reminder that "lung problem" does not mean "airway problem." PAH is a vessel disease, so the treatment is vasodilation and the organ that fails is the right heart; IPF is a membrane disease, so no bronchodilator helps and oxygen eventually stops working because the distance from alveolus to capillary — not the width of the tube — is the problem. Matching the failing structure to the drug class is the whole skill.

History that changes the plan.

Pack-years, vaping, occupational and environmental exposure, family history (AAT deficiency, CF, PAH). Asthma pattern — continuous, seasonal, activity, work, night waking, 4–8 weeks after a cold — and the four GINA control questions. Compare activity and dyspnea with a month and a year ago; ask about orthopnea, ADLs, sexual activity, sleep, and eating. Weigh and compare with previous weights.

Numbers and trends.

ABGs for gas exchange and acid-base, then oximetry for trend against the patient's usual saturation; PaCO₂ falls early and rises late. PFTs: 12% or more improvement after a bronchodilator diagnoses asthma; GOLD stages by FEV₁ % predicted; RV most affected; falling FEV₁/FVC. Polycythemia, electrolytes (low phosphate, potassium, calcium, magnesium weaken muscle), WBC and sputum culture, serum AAT, sweat chloride ≥60 mmol/L, right heart catheterisation for PAH, honeycombing on HRCT for IPF.

Right heart, nutrition, psychosocial.

Cor pulmonale: distended neck veins, dependent edema, enlarged tender liver, warm cyanotic extremities with bounding pulses, pulsations below the sternum. Clubbing and delayed capillary refill for chronic hypoxia. Weight, intake, early satiety, meal-related dyspnea, prealbumin. Isolation from dyspnea or embarrassment about coughing and sputum; cost of inhalers — many patients use them only during exacerbations; and the equity gap in COPD prevalence and access.

P — Plan.

Gas exchange is attained and maintained at the patient's usual baseline — not at a textbook value.
The patient coughs and clears secretions effectively, with a respiratory rate and rhythm appropriate to the activity, and a breathing pattern that decreases the work of breathing.
Body weight is achieved and maintained within 10% of ideal.
The patient reports increased endurance and decreased fatigue, and activity rises on a basis of balanced oxygen supply and demand.
Serious respiratory infection is avoided, with crowd avoidance and pneumonia and yearly influenza vaccination.
Before discharge the patient holds a personal action plan — asthma or COPD, green/yellow/red — demonstrates inhaler technique, states when to call and when to call 911, and has follow-up appointments already booked.
Why
"At the patient's usual baseline" is the most important six words in this chapter's outcomes. A patient whose chronic saturation is 89% and whose PaCO₂ chronically sits at 55 is at goal there; driving those numbers to normal is not a success but an intervention error. The plan is written against the patient's own physiology.

Independent.

Airway maintenance is the most important focus. Upright positioning with arms supported; out of bed to a chair 1 hour, two to three times a day; pursed-lip and diaphragmatic breathing taught while the patient is not dyspneic; controlled coughing on arising, before meals and before bed; fluids at least 2 L/day; small frequent high-calorie high-protein meals with the bronchodilator 30 minutes before; energy conservation and pacing, arms down, no talking while walking; assess the hospitalised COPD patient at least every 2 hours; suction only when needed, never routinely.


CHAPTER 25: INFECTIOUS RESPIRATORY CONDITIONS

On the exam: 5 questions — pneumonia.
Chapter 24 was structure failing slowly; this chapter is an organism filling the space where gas should be. Two concepts run in parallel the whole way through — gas exchange (the alveolus is full of exudate, so oxygen cannot cross) and infection (the organism is transmissible, so the patient is also a source). Every condition here is therefore two questions at once: how badly is diffusion impaired, and who else is at risk. The four that matter most are influenza, pneumonia, COVID-19 and tuberculosis, and the nursing work divides cleanly into prevent transmission, restore gas exchange, and finish the drug course.

4, What proves it. CXR infiltrate, sputum culture, blood cultures, CBC with left shift, AFB smear/culture for TB.
5, What targets it. Antibiotics or antivirals targeted to the organism, oxygen, fluids, mobility, isolation.

Down to the physiology.

How infection destroys diffusion — pathogens penetrate the airway mucosa and multiply in the alveolar spaces. White cells move in, capillaries leak, edema and exudate form in and around the alveoli, and the alveolar walls thicken. Red cells and fibrin follow. Gas now has to cross a thick, wet wall instead of a thin dry one: hypoxemia.
Then the lung stiffens — fibrin and edema reduce compliance and vital capacity, and atelectasis takes more units out of service. Capillary leak spreads the infection laterally; blood flowing past unventilated alveoli is shunted past the exchange surface unoxygenated.
Two spread patterns — lobar. pneumonia produces consolidation in a segment or a whole lobe; bronchopneumonia scatters patches around the bronchi. How far it goes depends on host defences — bacteria multiply quickly where immunity is compromised. An abscess that perforates the bronchial wall causes tissue necrosis.
Where it goes next — into the bloodstream is septicemia and sepsis; into the pleural cavity is empyema. Pneumonia is a frequent cause of sepsis, which is why a sepsis screening tool and a saturation with every set of vital signs are standing orders in this chapter.
Why the findings are the opposite of COPD's — fluid conducts sound better than air. So pneumonia gives increased fremitus, dull percussion, bronchial breath sounds over consolidated lung and crackles, while emphysema gives decreased fremitus and hyperresonance. Same stethoscope, opposite readings, because one lung is too full and the other too empty.
Why immunity is the whole prevention story — viral respiratory strains change slightly every year, so last year's antibodies are only partly effective: that is why adults get several colds a year and why influenza vaccination is annual. A pandemic organism is one humans have no ancestral immunity to, usually because it previously infected only birds or other animals and then mutated — 1918 influenza, 2009 H1N1, COVID-19.
Why
The single most tested idea in this chapter is that the older adult does not present typically. Fever and cough may be absent and the WBC count may stay normal until infection is severe, but hypoxemia is usually present — so the most common symptom of pneumonia in an older adult is a change in cognition, acute confusion from hypoxia. Waiting for the textbook picture greatly increases the risk of sepsis and death, which is why new confusion in an older adult is a respiratory assessment, not a neurological one.

Pneumonia — the gas exchange concept exemplar.

What it is — excess fluid in the lungs from an inflammatory process; infectious pneumonia adds a thick exudate of proteins and other particles that seriously reduces gas exchange. Inflammation sits in the interstitial spaces, the alveoli and often the bronchioles. Any organism can cause it — bacteria, viruses, mycoplasmas, fungi, rickettsiae, protozoa, helminths — and non-infectious causes are inhaled toxic gases, chemical fumes and smoke, or aspiration of water, food, fluid including saliva, and vomitus.
The organisms to name — most common bacterial cause Streptococcus pneumoniae; most common viral causes influenza A, influenza B and COVID-19. Drug-resistant S. pneumoniae (DRSP) is increasingly common, so local resistance patterns drive the antibiotic choice.
Three categories — community-acquired (CAP): acquired in the community, most common, peaks in late fall and winter, often as a complication of influenza; antibiotics are usually empirical, minimum 5 days, and in the emergency department the first dose should be given within 4 hours. Hospital-acquired (HAP): onset ≥48 hours after admission. Ventilator-associated (VAP): onset ≥48 hours after intubation, because the tube bypasses protective airway mechanisms and lets oropharyngeal and gastric secretions be aspirated — dental plaque raises the risk too. The old term HCAP is no longer used.
Preventing HAP and VAP — pulmonary hygiene and progressive ambulation, adequate hydration, aspiration risk screening with an evidence-based tool, monitoring for early sepsis, vigorous oral care, and hand hygiene, which is critical. The VAP bundle: head of bed at least 30 degrees, daily weaning assessment, DVT prophylaxis, an oral care regimen, stress ulcer prophylaxis, and suctioning as needed or continuous subglottal suction.
Risk factors — CAP: older adult; never had the pneumococcal vaccine or had it more than 5 years ago; no influenza vaccine last year; COVID-19 series and boosters not current; a chronic condition that reduces immunity; recent exposure to a respiratory viral or influenza infection; tobacco or alcohol use or heavy secondhand smoke. Nosocomial: older adult, chronic lung disease, gram-negative colonisation of mouth, throat and stomach, altered consciousness, a recent aspiration event, an endotracheal or nasogastric tube or tracheostomy, poor nutrition, reduced immunity, drugs that raise gastric pH (H₂ blockers, antacids) or alkaline tube feedings, and mechanical ventilation.
Prevention teaching — annual influenza vaccine, pneumococcal vaccine as recommended (PPSV23 and PCV15, or PCV20 alone for convenience; recommended over 65 and for adults 19–64 with risk factors), COVID-19 primary series and boosters; avoid crowds in flu season; cough, turn, move and deep breathe if mobility is limited; clean home respiratory equipment as instructed; avoid dust, secondhand smoke and aerosols; do not smoke or vape; rest, eat well and drink at least 3 L of non-alcoholic fluid a day unless restricted. Teach a patient with a cold or flu to be seen if fever lasts more than 24 hours, the illness lasts more than a week, or symptoms worsen.
Assessment — flushed cheeks and an anxious expression; chest pain or discomfort, myalgia, headache, chills, fever, cough, tachycardia, dyspnea, tachypnea, hemoptysis, sputum; severe chest muscle weakness from sustained coughing. The patient sits upright balancing on the hands — the tripod position. Crackles where fluid is in interstitial and alveolar areas, diminished breath sounds, wheeze if exudate narrows airways, bronchial breath sounds over consolidation, increased fremitus, dull percussion, and diminished or unequal chest expansion. Compare vital signs with baseline: hypotension with orthostatic change from vasodilation and dehydration, and a rapid weak pulse that may mean hypoxemia, dehydration, or impending sepsis and shock; dysrhythmias from cardiac tissue hypoxia.
Why each sign happens — raised rate and dyspnea from chemoreceptor and J-receptor stimulation, reduced compliance, anxiety and pain; hypoxemia from alveolar consolidation and pulmonary capillary shunting; cough from fluid in tracheal, bronchial and bronchiolar receptors; purulent, blood-tinged or rust-coloured sputum from capillary fluid and red cells moving into the alveoli; fever because phagocytes release pyrogens that reset the hypothalamus; pleuritic pain from inflammation of the parietal pleura hurting on inspiration.
Labs and imaging — sputum for Gram stain, culture and sensitivity (often the organism is never identified; a sputum trap during suctioning for the very ill); CBC for a raised WBC — often normal in older adults; blood cultures for bloodstream invasion; ABGs in the severely ill; electrolytes, BUN and creatinine (a high BUN and hypernatremia signal dehydration); a lactate for sepsis; influenza testing alongside CAP. Chest x-ray is the most common test but may not change for 2 or more days after symptoms — and in the older adult it is essential precisely because the symptoms are vague. Pulse oximetry for hypoxemia; thoracentesis if there is a pleural effusion.
Priority problems — 1. decreased gas exchange due to decreased diffusion at the alveolar-capillary membrane; 2. potential for airway obstruction due to inflammation with excessive secretions, fatigue and muscle weakness; 3. potential for sepsis due to organisms in a very vascular area plus reduced immunity.
Interventions — oxygen by cannula or mask (a confused patient may not tolerate a mask; check skin under the device and the elastic band, especially at the ears); incentive spirometry — sit up, exhale fully, seal the mouthpiece, long slow deep breath raising the piston as high as possible, hold 2–4 seconds, exhale slowly, 5–10 breaths per session every hour while awake, recording the volume; cough and deep breathe at least every 2 hours; at least 2 L of fluid daily to thin secretions, with intake and output, mucous membranes and skin turgor watched; bronchodilators for bronchospasm, inhaled or IV steroids for airway swelling, expectorants such as guaifenesin. Antiinfectives for every pneumonia except viral: about 5–7 days for uncomplicated CAP, up to 21 days for severely impaired immunity or HAP. Aspiration of acidic stomach contents can cause widespread inflammation leading to ARDS and permanent lung damage.
Transition and evaluation — finish the whole antibiotic course; expect fatigue, weakness and a residual cough for weeks, and reassure that complete recovery will occur; stay on one floor if stairs are tiring, consider a bedside commode; report returning or persistent chills, fever, cough, dyspnea, wheezing, hemoptysis, increased sputum, chest discomfort or increasing fatigue; avoid crowds, ill people and irritants; vaccinate after recovery. Outcomes: SaO₂ at least 95% or the patient's normal level, clear breath sounds, no fever with a normal WBC, and return to the pre-pneumonia health status. Readmission within 30 days is high, especially in older adults.
Why
Notice what the pneumonia intervention list actually is: oxygen, spirometry, coughing, fluid, and finishing the antibiotic. Three of those five are things the patient does — which is why teaching technique and completing the course is not discharge paperwork but the treatment itself. And aspiration is described as often silent: no cough, no gag, no complaint. That is why screening for aspiration risk is a routine assessment rather than a response to an event.

Seasonal influenza and pandemic infection.

Seasonal influenza — a highly contagious acute viral respiratory infection at any age, from strains of virus families A, B and C. Epidemics cause pneumonia and death especially in older adults, those with heart failure or chronic lung disease, and the immunocompromised. Most patients are treated at home.
The picture and the window — rapid onset of severe headache, muscle aches, fever, chills, fatigue and weakness, with sore throat, cough and watery nasal discharge; strain B can add nausea, vomiting and diarrhea. Adults are contagious 24 hours before symptoms and up to 5 days after they begin, and many feel fatigued for 1–2 weeks after the acute episode resolves.
Vaccination — annual, because the vaccine is rebuilt each year for the strains expected that season (trivalent or quadrivalent), usually IM with an intranasal alternative and a higher-dose quadrivalent for adults over 65. The CDC recommends no one product over another — the point is that everyone 6 months and older is vaccinated yearly. Nurses are patients' most important information source on vaccination, and being vaccinated protects patients from the nurse as well as the nurse from infection.
Containment teaching — thorough handwashing especially after nose blowing, sneezing, coughing, rubbing the eyes or touching the face; stay home from work, school and crowded places; cover mouth and nose with a tissue and dispose of it immediately; distance from others. Because most people cannot wash immediately, the CDC technique is to sneeze or cough into the upper sleeve rather than the hand — droplets on hands contaminate surfaces.
Diagnosis and drugs — in a community with known cases, diagnosis is usually clinical. The rapid influenza diagnostic test has a high false-negative rate — treat if influenza is suspected even when it is negative; the preferred test is RT-PCR (1–8 hours), which distinguishes A from B. Viral infection does not respond to antibiotics. Neuraminidase inhibitors — oseltamivir, zanamivir, peramivir, baloxavir — block the enzyme the virus uses to penetrate respiratory cells and may shorten illness, but must be started within 24–48 hours of symptom onset; they reduce hospitalisation in patients at increased risk. Otherwise: rest several days, increase fluids, saline gargles for throat pain, antihistamines for rhinorrhea.
Pandemic respiratory infection — an infection with an organism to which most humans have no immunity and which can spread globally, usually a bird or animal virus that mutated into human transmissibility. Prevention is everyone's responsibility: global, national and state surveillance, early case recognition, community and personal quarantine, social distancing, stockpiled vaccine released to the affected area, stopping non-essential public activity — gatherings, schools, services, shopping, much employment — halting travel, and households living on at least 2 weeks of stockpiled food, water and medication per person.
Care priorities — supportive measures plus preventing spread. Ask every patient about travel in the last 14 days to an outbreak area; if yes, mask the patient and isolate until assessment is complete. Until the organism and its transmission routes are known, use Airborne, Droplet and Contact Precautions together. Stable patients recover at home with rest, fluids and acetaminophen for fever or myalgia; an antiviral such as oseltamivir may be prescribed within 48 hours. Oxygen when hypoxia, breathlessness or a change in cognition occurs; bronchodilation and secretion clearance; intubation and mechanical ventilation if hypoxemia does not respond; antibiotics only for a bacterial pneumonia complicating the influenza.
Why
The 24-to-48-hour antiviral window is the reason a "flu" phone call is a triage decision rather than reassurance. By day 5 of symptoms, oseltamivir has little left to offer and the honest answer is that the window has closed — which is also the strongest argument the nurse has for next year's vaccine. And the contagious period starting a day before symptoms is why containment teaching cannot wait for a diagnosis.

Rhinosinusitis, peritonsillar abscess, anthrax and endemic infection.

Rhinosinusitis — inflammation of the mucous membranes of one or more sinuses, usually with rhinitis and most often the common cold. Anything that blocks sinus drainage can cause it: deviated septum, polyps or tumors, inhaled pollutants or cocaine, allergies, facial trauma, dental infection. Even a non-infectious start — seasonal allergy — blocks secretion flow, and the trapped secretions then become infected. Most episodes are viral and develop in the maxillary and frontal sinuses. Complications: cellulitis, abscess and meningitis.
Recognising and treating it — pain over the cheek radiating to the teeth, tenderness to percussion over the sinuses, referred pain to the temple or back of the head, and facial pain worse on bending forward. Bacterial infection adds purulent drainage with postnasal drip, sore throat, fever, swelling, fatigue, dental pain and ear pressure. Manage as an outpatient: decongestants, antihistamines and intranasal steroid spray, antipyretics and analgesics; broad-spectrum antibiotics for bacterial infection, sometimes nasal or systemic steroids. Supportive care — humidification, nasal irrigation, moist heat over the sinuses, rest, more fluid, sleeping with the head elevated and avoiding cigarette smoke. Complete the whole antibiotic course even when symptoms subside, to eradicate the organism and prevent resistance.
First-generation antihistamines in older adults — often inappropriate: reduced drug clearance, higher risk of confusion, and anticholinergic effects such as dry mouth and constipation. Avoid chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine and hydroxyzine, and teach older adults not to self-medicate with them.
Peritonsillar abscess — a rare complication of acute bacterial tonsillitis: infection spreads from the tonsil into surrounding tissue and the abscess can grow large enough to obstruct the airway. Pus behind the tonsil swells one side of the throat and pushes the uvula toward the unaffected side; severe throat pain radiates to the ear or teeth, with a muffled voice, fever, difficulty swallowing, trismus (spasm and pain of the chewing muscles), difficulty breathing, bad breath and swollen nodes on the affected side. Most are treated as outpatients with antibiotics, sometimes steroids for swelling, and drainage if needed — and the teaching is to finish the antibiotics and come to the emergency department immediately if drooling or stridor appear.
Inhalation anthrax — a bacterial infection with the gram-positive Bacillus anthracis, which lives as a spore in soil where grazing animals live. Most natural cases are cutaneous; inhaled, the disease is nearly 100% fatal without treatment. It is an occupational hazard for veterinarians, farmers and others handling animal wool, hides, bone meal and skin — so a case in someone without occupational risk may be bioterrorism and is reported to hospital authorities immediately. Inhaled spores enter white cells, leave their capsules, replicate and release toxins; massive edema, hemorrhage and lung cell destruction follow, and infected white cells spread the organism to nodes and blood, producing bacteremia, sepsis and meningitis. The lethal toxins are the most common cause of death.
Anthrax's two stages — symptoms may take up to 8 weeks to appear. Prodromal: low-grade fever, fatigue, mild chest pain, a dry harsh cough, and — distinctively — no upper respiratory symptoms, no sore throat or rhinitis; mediastinal widening may show on x-ray. The patient usually starts to feel better in 2–4 days, and antibiotics started here mean a high likelihood of survival. Fulminant: a sudden severe onset after that apparent improvement — respiratory distress, hematemesis, dyspnea, diaphoresis, stridor, chest pain, cyanosis, high fever, hemorrhagic mediastinitis, pleural effusion, hypotension and septic shock, with hemorrhagic meningitis as it spreads. Death often occurs within 24–36 hours even with antibiotics started at this stage. Treatment combines ciprofloxacin, doxycycline, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin and clindamycin, with the antitoxins obiltoxaximab and raxibacumab added once symptoms and diagnosis are established.
Endemic and geographic infection — organisms much more common in a particular geographic area, though the actual incidence stays low because residents develop some immunity; most are spore-forming fungi. Hantavirus pulmonary syndrome from rodent urine, droppings and saliva in the southwest US, Mexico and Central America — supportive care and oxygen, ventilation in severe cases, 38% fatal. Aspergillosis from indoor and outdoor mold in damp areas. Blastomycosis from moist soil and decomposing wood and leaves around the Great Lakes and the Ohio and Mississippi River valleys. Coccidioidomycosis from soil in the southwest and far western US, Mexico, Central and South America. Cryptococcosis from trees and the soil beneath them on the US Pacific coast and southern British Columbia. Histoplasmosis from soil with large amounts of bird and bat droppings in the central and eastern US.
Why they are missed, and how they are treated — all of them resemble influenza or pneumonia — fever, cough, headache, muscle aches, chest pain, night sweats — and are often misdiagnosed; none are contagious person to person. Heavy exposure comes from digging, farming, construction that disturbs soil, dust storms, tornadoes and flooding, and demolition or reconstruction that releases spores trapped in walls. Always ask a patient with respiratory infection symptoms whether they have visited an endemic region. Older adults, pregnant patients and the immunocompromised get more severe disease, and chronic fungal infection is possible. Moderate disease takes oral antifungals for weeks to months; severe disease starts with IV antifungals including amphotericin and then long-term oral therapy, with influenza- and pneumonia-style supportive care throughout.
Why
Two lessons hide in this card. First, the anthrax teaching point generalises: teach any patient with a lower respiratory infection to watch for a setback after they think they are getting well, and to seek care immediately if breathlessness starts — improvement followed by collapse is the pattern that kills. Second, geography is diagnostic data. The same fever and cough means something different in the Ohio River valley, the Arizona desert and a building under demolition, so the travel and exposure question is not small talk.

Exposure history — ask first.

Travel in the last 14 days to an outbreak area; travel to or from countries with high TB incidence and country of origin; previous TB test results and BCG vaccination; household illness and any hospital or long-term care contact in 30 days; visits to endemic regions; occupational exposure to animal wool, hides, bone meal or skin; vaccination status for influenza, pneumococcus and COVID-19. In pneumonia also document age, living, work and school environment, diet, exercise and sleep, swallowing problems, a nasogastric tube, tobacco and alcohol, illicit drug use, and home respiratory equipment with its cleaning routine.

The atypical presentations.

Older adult pneumonia: weakness, fatigue and falls, lethargy, acute confusion as the most common symptom, poor appetite — often with no fever, no cough and a normal WBC, but with hypoxemia. Sepsis: a rapid weak pulse, hypotension with orthostatic change, dysrhythmias, and a screening tool used deliberately. TB: weeks to months of fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, low-grade fever, night sweats and blood-streaked sputum. Anthrax: no upper respiratory symptoms, and improvement for 2–4 days before collapse. Aspiration is often silent.

D — Diagnose & prioritize.

Pneumonia. 1. Decreased gas exchange due to decreased diffusion at the alveolar-capillary membrane. 2. Potential for airway obstruction due to inflammation with excessive secretions, fatigue and muscle weakness. 3. Potential for sepsis due to organisms in a very vascular area and reduced immunity.
COVID-19. 1. Potential to transmit infection, because the disease is highly communicable. 2. Potential for reduced oxygenation. 3. Potential for progression to severe or critical infection.
Tuberculosis. Potential to transmit infection by the airborne route; decreased gas exchange; inadequate nutrition and weight loss; and potential for non-adherence to a six-to-nine-month regimen — which is also a potential for drug-resistant disease in the patient and the community.
Notice the ordering difference. In pneumonia the patient's diffusion comes first; in COVID-19 and TB transmission comes first, because the organism's reach is the larger harm and the window to contain it is now.
Why
"Potential for sepsis" sits third in pneumonia but it is the problem that kills: when sepsis occurs with pneumonia the risk of death is high. It ranks below the airway because gas exchange and secretions are the problems you act on continuously, while sepsis is the one you screen for relentlessly — a sepsis tool plus a saturation with every set of vital signs. Ranking it third does not mean watching it less.

P — Plan.

Gas exchange and oxygenation are adequate — SaO₂ at least 95% or the patient's normal level in pneumonia; 95% or greater in COVID-19.
The airway stays patent, evidenced by the absence of crackles and wheezes on auscultation.
The patient is free of the invading organism — no fever, a normal WBC — and returns to the pre-illness health status.
The patient does not transmit the infection to others: isolation or precautions followed, household members tested in TB, contacts encouraged to test.
The patient does not progress — no severe or critical COVID-19, no post-COVID condition, no sepsis, no empyema, no ARDS.
Before discharge the patient states the whole drug course and why it is finished even when feeling well, the warning signs that mean return, and the vaccinations due once recovered.
Why
The outcome "does not transmit" is the one that makes this chapter different from every other respiratory chapter. It is measured in other people's charts, not this one — contacts tested, household screened, staff uninfected — and it is achieved almost entirely by nursing actions: precautions, technique, teaching and timing.

Dependent.

Oxygen by cannula or mask; antiinfectives for every pneumonia except viral — 5–7 days uncomplicated CAP, up to 21 days for HAP or reduced immunity, first ED dose within 4 hours; bronchodilators for bronchospasm, inhaled or IV steroids for swelling, guaifenesin; neuraminidase inhibitors within 24–48 hours of influenza onset; nirmatrelvir-ritonavir, remdesivir or bebtelovimab for at-risk COVID-19; four-drug TB therapy for 6–9 months or the 4-month regimen, bedaquiline with ECG monitoring for MDR TB; antifungals, oral for weeks to months or IV amphotericin in severe endemic disease; antitoxin plus combination antibiotics in anthrax.

E — Evaluate.

Gas exchange is adequate with SaO₂ at least 95% or the patient's usual level; breath sounds clear of crackles and wheezes; the patient is free of infection with no fever and a normal WBC; no one else has been infected; and the patient returns to the pre-illness health status. In TB, three consecutive negative sputum cultures — cultures are usually negative after 3 months of effective therapy — and every household contact tested.
Unmet looks like: new or increasing confusion in an older adult; a rapid weak pulse, hypotension or a rising lactate — sepsis; dyspnea at rest or worsening dyspnea in COVID-19, which can reach ARDS within 2 days; chest pain, edema, hemoptysis or orthopnea suggesting embolism, heart failure or pericarditis; a setback after apparent improvement; persistent fever, cough, sputum or fatigue that does not resolve; drooling or stridor in peritonsillar abscess; vision or liver warning signs on TB therapy; and readmission within 30 days, which is common after pneumonia in older adults.
Set expectations so recovery is not read as failure: fatigue, weakness and a residual cough last weeks after pneumonia, influenza fatigue lasts 1–2 weeks, acute COVID-19 can run up to 4 weeks, and post-COVID condition can last 2 months or more. Tell the patient the course, and reassure that complete recovery does occur.
Why
The recovery timeline belongs in evaluation because patients judge themselves against it. A patient three weeks past pneumonia who still cannot climb the stairs concludes something has gone wrong, stops the follow-up, and either despairs or presents to the emergency department. Telling them in advance that weeks of fatigue are expected — while naming exactly which findings are not — is what makes the difference between a worried call and a missed relapse.


CHAPTER 27: ASSESSMENT OF THE CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM

On the exam: 3 questions — cardiovascular changes associated with aging.
The whole chapter is one equation and its consequences. Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume, and stroke volume is set by preload, afterload, and contractility. Every assessment finding, lab, and test in this chapter is measuring one of those terms — and every cardiac drug in the chapters that follow moves one of them on purpose.

Connection Map. How this chapter fits together.

1, Normal physiology. CO = HR × SV; SV depends on preload, afterload and contractility.
2, What fails. One lever fails: rate, filling, resistance or squeeze.
3, What the nurse sees. Chest pain, dyspnea, edema, JVD, cool skin, weak pulses, low urine output.
4, What proves it. Troponin (serial), BNP, lipids, ECG, echo (EF), stress test, catheterization.
5, What targets it. Drugs that move a lever: beta blockers (rate), diuretics (preload), ACE inhibitors (afterload), inotropes (contractility).
6, If it worsens. Low CO leads to organ hypoperfusion leads to shock.
7, Emergency trigger. Chest pain with diaphoresis, groin bleeding with hypotension, absent distal pulse after catheterization, urine less than 30 mL/hr.
Cause and effect. Myocyte death leads to troponin released leads to rises 3–6 h leads to serial draws detect a new MI leads to one early normal value does not rule it out

Down to the physiology.

Preload — the stretch on the ventricle at the end of filling. More volume means more stretch means a stronger contraction (Starling's law) until the muscle is overstretched and output falls. Raised by fluid overload and heart failure; lowered by dehydration, hemorrhage, diuretics, and venous dilators.
Afterload — the resistance the ventricle must push against. Raised by hypertension, aortic stenosis, and vasoconstriction; lowered by vasodilators and ACE inhibitors. High afterload means the heart works harder for less output.
Contractility — the intrinsic squeeze, independent of stretch. Improved by positive inotropes (digoxin, dobutamine), reduced by ischemia, acidosis, hypoxia, and negative inotropes such as beta blockers.
Conduction — SA node (60–100) leads to AV node (40–60) leads to bundle of His leads to bundle branches leads to Purkinje fibers (20–40). Each level is a backup pacemaker at a slower rate, which is why a junctional or ventricular escape rhythm is both a rescue and a warning.
Coronary perfusion happens in diastole, when the muscle is relaxed. That is why tachycardia is dangerous in coronary disease: a faster rate shortens diastole and steals the heart's own blood supply at the moment demand is highest.
Aging changes — stiffer vessels and valves, reduced maximum heart rate and response to catecholamines, more fibrosis in the conduction system, and lower baroreceptor sensitivity, which causes orthostatic hypotension and falls.
Why
If you can say which of the four levers a finding or a drug is moving, you can predict the consequence without memorizing it. A patient with crackles and jugular distention has too much preload; a patient whose blood pressure is 190/100 with pulmonary edema is failing against afterload; a patient in ventricular tachycardia at 180 has no time left in diastole to fill or to perfuse the coronaries. Same equation, three different priorities.

The cardiac assessment, in order.

History — chest pain (PQRSTU), dyspnea and orthopnea, palpitations, fatigue, syncope, edema, nocturia, claudication, weight change, family history of early cardiac death, pack-years, diet, activity, and drugs including over-the-counter and herbals.
Inspect — color, clubbing, edema (and whether it is pitting), jugular venous distention at 45°, skin temperature and hair distribution on the legs, capillary refill.
Palpate — pulses in pairs for rate, rhythm, and equality (0 absent to 4+ bounding), the apical impulse at the fifth intercostal space midclavicular line, edema, and temperature.
Auscultate — aortic, pulmonic, Erb point, tricuspid, mitral. S1 (AV valves close, start of systole), S2 (semilunar valves close), S3 (early diastolic, volume overload — expected in the young, heart failure in the older adult), S4 (late diastolic, stiff ventricle), murmur, pericardial friction rub.
Apical-radial pulse — count simultaneously for a full minute; the difference is the pulse deficit, meaning beats that do not generate a peripheral pulse.
Blood pressure — both arms, correct cuff size, orthostatic set (lying, sitting, standing after 1–3 minutes). Paradoxical BP: a systolic fall of more than 10 mm Hg on inspiration suggests cardiac tamponade or severe lung disease.
Why
A pulse deficit is the bedside proof that electrical activity is outrunning mechanical output — the heart fires, but the ventricle has not filled enough to open the aortic valve. That is exactly what atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular response does, which is why the two findings travel together on exams and why the apical rate, not the radial, is the one you chart.

Structure, coronary supply, and the cardiac cycle.

The valves — the AV valves (tricuspid right, mitral/bicuspid left) separate atria from ventricles; in diastole they funnel blood downward, in systole they close to prevent regurgitation. The semilunar valves (pulmonic, aortic) stop blood returning to the ventricles during diastole.
Where the coronaries begin — on the aorta just beyond the aortic valve. Everything feeding the left heart comes from the left main coronary artery; the right coronary artery perfuses the right heart and the inferior wall of the left ventricle.
The branches — the LAD descends over the anterior wall and apex, supplying much of the left ventricle, the septum, chordae tendineae, and papillary muscle. The LCX runs to the lateral and posterior left ventricle and left atrium and supplies the SA node in about half of people. The RCA supplies the right atrium and ventricle and the inferior left ventricle, the SA node in about half of people, and the AV node in nearly everyone.
Why timing matters — coronary flow occurs mainly during diastole, when coronary vascular resistance is lowest, and needs a MAP of at least 60 mm Hg; 60–70 mm Hg is required to perfuse the kidneys and brain.
Diastole versus systole — diastole is relaxation and filling and occupies about two-thirds of the cardiac cycle; systole is contraction and emptying. Anything that shortens diastole steals both filling time and coronary perfusion.
How the muscle contracts — calcium released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and the blood diffuses into the sarcomere, linking actin and myosin into force-generating cross-bridges that slide and shorten it. Relaxation follows when calcium is pumped back, filaments disengage, and the sarcomere lengthens.
Output and index — CO in adults is 3–6 L/min; the cardiac index (CO divided by body surface area) corrects for body size, normal 2.8–4.2 L/min/m&sup2;. Resting rate 60–100, and every increase in rate raises myocardial oxygen demand.
Rate control — the vagus nerve slows; sympathetic stimulation and circulating catecholamines speed and strengthen. Beta blockers work by blocking that fight-or-flight pattern.
Starling's law and its limit — more filling produces a stronger contraction within limits; excessive end-diastolic volume and pressure reduces output. Impedance, the peripheral part of afterload, is the pressure needed to open the aortic valve, set by aortic compliance and systemic vascular resistance (viscosity plus arteriolar constriction).
Contractility — raised by sympathetic stimulation, calcium release, and positive inotropes; lowered by hypoxia and acidemia.
Why
Knowing which artery feeds which node turns a rhythm finding into a location. The RCA supplies the AV node in almost everyone, so an inferior wall infarction characteristically arrives with bradycardia and AV block, while anterior infarctions from the LAD threaten pump function instead. Same disease, different artery, different complication to watch for.

Blood pressure regulation and the vascular system.

The pressure gradient — about 80–100 mm Hg in large arteries, roughly 25 mm Hg at the capillaries, and 0–5 mm Hg entering the right atrium. Volume, ventricular contraction, and vascular tone maintain it.
Three regulating mechanisms — the autonomic nervous system responding to baroreceptors and chemoreceptors; the kidneys, activating renin-angiotensin-aldosterone when flow falls; and the endocrine system, releasing catecholamines, kinins, serotonin, and histamine.
Baroreceptors — in the aortic arch and at the origin of the internal carotids. Stretched by rising pressure, they inhibit the vasomotor center in the pons and medulla and pressure falls.
Chemoreceptors — peripheral ones in the carotids and aortic arch respond mainly to hypoxemia and trigger vasoconstriction; central ones respond to hypercapnia and acidosis, and the direct CO&#8322; effect on the CNS is ten times stronger than the peripheral hypoxic effect.
Stretch receptors — in the vena cava and right atrium. In hypovolemia they sense low volume, send fewer impulses, and the sympathetic system raises rate and constricts peripheral vessels.
Renal and hormonal backup — falling renal flow means sodium and water retention, RAAS activation, vasoconstriction, and rising pressure; ADH (vasopressin) from the posterior pituitary also regulates vascular volume.
Other influences — excitement, pain, and anger raise pressure and rate through sympathetic arousal. In hypothermia tissues need less and pressure falls; in hyperthermia demand rises and both pressure and rate climb.
The venous system — returns blood to the right heart through deep veins, a parallel superficial circulation, and connecting veins, with flow directed toward the deep system. Veins absorb large volume shifts with little pressure change, which is what lets them take IV fluids and transfusions and hold pressure during blood loss.
Valves and the muscle pump — veins (except the smallest and largest) have valves directing flow back to the heart, and skeletal muscle pushes it forward. The vena cavae are valveless and wide enough for easy return.
Gravity — upright posture raises capillary hydrostatic pressure and delays venous return; lying down lowers it in the legs. That one fact explains both dependent edema and why elevation relieves venous discomfort.
Why
Aging degrades these mechanisms predictably, and the assessment follows: baroreceptors become less sensitive, so the reflex that should defend pressure on standing fires too slowly. That is the mechanism behind orthostatic and postprandial hypotension in older adults, and why "change position slowly" is a physiological intervention rather than generic advice.

Chest discomfort — telling the sources apart.

The rule — chest pain is considered ischemic until proven otherwise. Ask about "discomfort," "heaviness," "pressure," or "indigestion" — many patients deny pain and describe one of those instead.
Angina — sudden, usually with exertion, emotion, or temperature extremes. Squeezing, viselike, usually left chest, substernal, may spread across the chest and back or down the arms. Typically under 15 minutes, relieved by rest, nitrates, or oxygen.
Myocardial infarction — sudden, without precipitating factors, often in early morning. Intense stabbing or viselike pressure, severe, substernal, spreading to arms, jaw, back, or neck. Continuous, not relieved by rest or position change; relieved by morphine and cardiac drugs.
Pericarditis — sudden, sharp and stabbing, substernal spreading to the left side or back, intermittent, relieved by sitting upright, analgesia, or antiinflammatories.
Pleuropulmonary — variable onset, a moderate ache worse on inspiration, over the lung fields, continuous until the cause is treated.
Esophageal-gastric — squeezing heartburn, substernal, may spread to shoulders or abdomen, relieved by antacids, food, or sitting up.
Anxiety — variable, often with stress or fatigue; dull ache to sharp stabbing, sometimes with finger numbness, poorly localized, lasting a few minutes.
The female presentation — many women feel discomfort or indigestion rather than chest pain, and often present with a triad: indigestion or abdominal fullness, chronic fatigue despite adequate rest, and an inability to catch their breath. Others describe aching, choking, strangling, tingling, or constricting. Patients with severe neuropathy may have no symptom but shortness of breath despite major ischemia.
Dyspnea, graded by what it takes — dyspnea on exertion is often the earliest sign of heart failure and may be a woman's only symptom. Orthopnea is measured in pillows and relieved within minutes of sitting up. PND comes hours after lying down as fluid redistributes from the legs: the patient wakes abruptly feeling suffocated and panicked, sits up and dangles the legs, and it may take 20 minutes to pass.
Fatigue — worse in the evening when it comes from low output. Ask whether they can do what they did a year ago, or what others their age can do; patients quietly shrink their activity and do not notice until asked.
Palpitations, weight, syncope — palpitations may be dysrhythmic or come from anxiety, fatigue, insomnia, hyperthyroidism, caffeine, nicotine, or alcohol. A gain of 2.2 lb (1 kg) equals about 1 L of retained fluid, and 10–15 lb can accumulate before edema is visible — so ask about tight shoes, sock indentations, and tight rings. Syncope is brief loss of consciousness from reduced cerebral perfusion; in older adults it can come from carotid sinus hypersensitivity triggered by turning the head, shrugging, or the Valsalva maneuver.
Extremity pain — two sources. Intermittent claudication is moderate to severe cramping in the legs or buttocks with walking, relieved by rest or lowering the limb — arterial. Pain from prolonged standing or sitting, relieved by elevation, is venous insufficiency.
NYHA functional classification — I: cardiac disease, no limitation, ordinary activity causes no symptoms. II: slight limitation, comfortable at rest, ordinary activity causes symptoms. III: marked limitation, comfortable at rest, less than ordinary activity causes symptoms. IV: unable to do any activity without discomfort, symptoms possible at rest.
Why
Notice what actually separates angina from infarction: not the words the patient uses, but whether rest and a nitrate end it. Angina is demand outrunning supply and stops when demand falls; infarction is tissue dying and does not care that the patient sat down. That is why "relieved by rest" is the most decision-changing question in the assessment.

The physical exam — skin, pulses, sounds, numbers.

Where to assess perfusion — nail beds, mucous membranes, and conjunctival mucosa, because small vessels sit near the surface there. Adequate perfusion in light skin looks pink and warm; in dark skin, warm with a healthy glow. Decreased perfusion is cool, pale or ash gray, and moist.
Cyanosis across skin tones — in Brown or Black adults cyanosis appears as a graying of skin and mucous membranes; in White skin as a bluish or darkened discoloration. Reduced perfusion can make brown skin look yellow-brown and dark brown or Black skin look ash gray.
Central versus peripheral cyanosis — central (bluish conjunctivae, mouth, tongue) means poor arterial oxygenation in the lungs or a right-to-left shunt. Peripheral (nail beds, earlobes, lips, toes) means vasoconstriction from low output or high tissue extraction. Rubor replacing pallor in a dependent foot suggests arterial insufficiency.
Clubbing — from chronic tissue oxygen deprivation. The normal nail bed angle is 160 degrees; with clubbing it straightens to 180 degrees and the nail base becomes spongy.
Edema — bilateral leg edema suggests heart failure or chronic venous insufficiency; abdominal plus leg edema suggests heart disease with cirrhosis; edema in one extremity suggests venous thrombosis or lymphatic blockage. Dependent sacral edema appears in bedbound patients, and amlodipine is a common drug cause. Document location precisely in centimeters from a landmark and note whether it pits — the 1+ to 4+ grades are imprecise and unreliable.
Blood pressure — hypertension is 140/90 or higher (JNC 8), with AHA/ACC suggesting below 130/80 for everyone. Below 90/60 may not perfuse tissue; in shock Korotkoff sounds may be absent, so palpate (systolic only), use Doppler, or an arterial line.
Orthostatic technique — supine first, remain supine at least 3 minutes, change to sitting or standing, then wait at least 1 minute before auscultating and counting the radial pulse. Positive is a fall over 20 mm Hg systolic or 10 mm Hg diastolic with a 10–20% rise in heart rate. Causes: cardiovascular drugs, low volume, prolonged bed rest, aging, nervous system disorders.
Pulse pressure and paradoxical pressure — pulse pressure is an indirect measure of cardiac output; narrowed is rarely normal and comes from high resistance or low stroke volume (heart failure, hypovolemia, shock, mitral disease), while increased occurs with slow rates, aortic regurgitation, atherosclerosis, hypertension, and aging. Paradoxical BP is a fall over 10 mm Hg on inspiration (normal 3–10) and suggests tamponade, constrictive pericarditis, or pulmonary hypertension.
ABI and TBPI — cuff above the malleolus, Doppler at the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial, the higher divided by the higher brachial. Normal 1.00 or higher; under 0.9 indicates vascular disease and under 0.5 severe compromise. The toe-brachial index assesses perfusion in the feet and toes.
Pulses and bruits — assess major peripheral pulses head-to-toe with side-to-side comparison for presence, amplitude, contour, rhythm, rate, and equality. A weak pulse occurs with hypovolemia, aortic stenosis, and low output; a bounding (hyperkinetic) pulse with high output (exercise, sepsis, thyrotoxicosis) or sympathetic arousal (pain, fever, anxiety). A bruit may appear once a vessel narrows by 50% or more — it does not indicate severity, and once blocked 90% or more the bruit often disappears.
Jugular venous distention — reflects filling volume and right-heart pressure. Usually volume overload or right ventricular failure; also tricuspid disease, pulmonary hypertension, tamponade, constrictive pericarditis, hypervolemia, and superior vena cava obstruction.
Apical impulse — the point of maximal impulse belongs at the left fifth intercostal space, midclavicular line. Spanning more than one space or shifting lateral to that line suggests left ventricular hypertrophy. Visible movement over the aortic, pulmonic, or tricuspid areas is abnormal.
Auscultation technique — progress systematically from the aortic area to the apex. The diaphragm pressed firmly picks up high-frequency sounds — S1, S2, high-frequency murmurs; the bell held lightly picks up low-frequency gallops and murmurs. If sounds are hard to hear, have the patient lean forward or roll onto the left side.
S1 and S2 in detail — S1 (AV valves closing) is softer, longer, low-pitched, best at the lower left sternal border or apex, falling just after the QRS; accentuated by exercise, hyperthyroidism, and mitral stenosis, softened by mitral regurgitation and heart failure. S2 (semilunar valves) is shorter, higher-pitched, best at the base. Splitting widens on inspiration with increased venous return and narrows on expiration; pathologic splitting suggests pulmonary hypertension or stenosis, right ventricular failure, or conduction disturbance.
Gallops — S3 (ventricular gallop) arises in rapid passive filling of a noncompliant ventricle and is normal under age 35 but abnormal after, signaling reduced compliance or early heart failure. S4 (atrial gallop) comes at the end of diastole during active filling, with hypertension, anemia, hypertrophy, MI, aortic or pulmonic stenosis, pulmonary emboli, and aging. Both are best heard with the bell at the apex, patient on the left side, during expiration.
Murmurs — turbulent flow, classified by timing: systolic (aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation) between S1 and S2; diastolic (mitral stenosis, aortic regurgitation) between S2 and S1. Graded I very faint to VI audible with the stethoscope off the chest, with a palpable thrill from grade IV up. Describe location, radiation, crescendo or decrescendo configuration, quality (harsh, blowing, whistling, rumbling, squeaking), and pitch.
Pericardial friction rub — arises from the pericardial sac with the movement of the heart; usually transient, and a sign of inflammation, infection, or infiltration — pericarditis after MI, tamponade, or thoracotomy.
Key labs — cholesterol under 200, LDL under 130, HDL above 45 (male) or 55 (female), triglycerides 40–160 (male) or 35–135 (female), VLDL 7–32, HDL/LDL ratio 3:1, CRP under 1.0 mg/dL, troponin T under 0.1 and troponin I under 0.03 ng/mL.
Psychosocial — cardiac illness is experienced as a life crisis, with fears of pain, disability, dependence, and changed family dynamics. Ask "what do you understand about what happened to you?" and "what does that mean to you?" Denial is a normal defense — it becomes maladaptive only when it produces nonadherence.
Why
The bruit is the finding most often misread, and its physics explain why. Turbulence needs a narrowing and enough flow to be turbulent — so a 50% lesion sings and a 90% lesion goes quiet. A silent carotid is not a reassuring carotid, which is why the bruit supports the picture but never grades the disease.


CHAPTER 28: DYSRHYTHMIAS

On the exam: 5 questions — ECG recording and ECG analysis.
A rhythm strip is not a picture of the heart beating — it is a picture of electricity moving. The single question that makes this chapter tractable is: does this rhythm produce a pulse and a pressure? Everything else — naming it, reading intervals, choosing the drug or the shock — follows from that.

Connection Map. How this chapter fits together.

1, Normal physiology. SA node fires, impulse travels AV node leads to bundle leads to Purkinje; each beat fills and empties the ventricles.
2, What fails. Impulse forms too fast, too slow, chaotically, or is blocked.
3, What the nurse sees. Palpitations, dizziness, syncope, hypotension, chest pain, irregular pulse.
4, What proves it. Rhythm strip: rate, regularity, P for every QRS, PR, QRS width; K⁺ and Mg²⁺.
5, What targets it. Match drug to mechanism: adenosine (AV node), amiodarone (repolarization), atropine (vagal block), shock for pulseless rhythms.
6, If it worsens. Low CO leads to stroke (AF clots), arrest (VT/VF).
7, Emergency trigger. No pulse, unstable with pulse (hypotension, altered LOC, chest pain), torsades.
Cause and effect. Atrial fibrillation leads to no atrial kick and blood stasis leads to lower CO and clot formation leads to stroke risk leads to rate control plus anticoagulation
Cardioversion vs defibrillation
Synchronized for rhythms with a pulse; unsynchronized for pulseless VT/VF.
Why the drug makes sense
Digoxin + low K⁺ leads to more drug binding at Na⁺/K⁺ pump leads to toxicity and dysrhythmias leads to check K⁺ and apical pulse first.

Monitor
Controlled rate, stable BP, no symptoms.
Act now
Pulseless rhythm, symptomatic brady/tachy, torsades.

1, Rate. Regular rhythm: 1500 ÷ small boxes between R waves, or 300 ÷ large boxes. Irregular: count QRS complexes in a 6-second strip × 10..


3, P waves. Is there one P for every QRS, upright and identical? No P at all, sawtooth, or chaotic baseline all name the atrial problem..


4, PR interval. 0.12–0.20 s. Long = first-degree block; lengthening then dropping = Mobitz I; dropping without warning = Mobitz II; no relationship at all = third-degree..


5, QRS duration. 0.06–0.10 s. Narrow means the impulse came from above the ventricles; wide means it started in the ventricle or took an abnormal path..


The rhythms, and what each one does to output.

Sinus bradycardia (less than 60, otherwise normal) — fine in an athlete or during sleep. Symptomatic (dizziness, hypotension, confusion, chest pain): atropine, then transcutaneous pacing. Look for the cause — vagal stimulation, beta blockers, digoxin, inferior MI, raised intracranial pressure.
Sinus tachycardia (greater than 100, otherwise normal) — almost always a symptom: pain, fever, hypovolemia, anxiety, hypoxia, anemia, stimulants. Treat the cause, not the number; it shortens diastole and reduces coronary filling.
Premature atrial complexes — early, abnormal P wave. Usually benign; caffeine, stress, stimulants, or early heart failure.
Supraventricular tachycardia — narrow, regular, 150–220, P waves buried. Stable: vagal maneuvers, then adenosine (push fast, warn the patient about a few seconds of asystole and a flushing sensation). Unstable: synchronized cardioversion.
Atrial fibrillation — no organized P waves, irregularly irregular, chaotic atrial rate of 350–600. The atria quiver instead of contracting: loss of the atrial kick costs up to 20–30% of output, and stagnant blood forms clots. Management is rate control (beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, digoxin), rhythm control (antidysrhythmics, cardioversion, ablation, maze procedure), and anticoagulation.
Premature ventricular complexes — early, wide, bizarre QRS with no preceding P. Concerning when frequent, multifocal, in runs, or falling on the T wave. Check potassium and magnesium, oxygenation, and ischemia.
Ventricular tachycardia — three or more consecutive PVCs, wide and fast. With a pulse and stable: antidysrhythmics (amiodarone), prepare for synchronized cardioversion. Pulseless: CPR and defibrillation.
Torsades de pointes — twisting polymorphic VT caused by a prolonged QT. Treatment is IV magnesium, plus removing the offending drug.
Ventricular fibrillation — chaotic, no identifiable waveform, no output at all. CPR, defibrillate immediately, epinephrine, amiodarone.
Asystole — flat line. Confirm in two leads and check the patient and the electrodes; CPR and epinephrine. Not shockable.
AV blocks — first-degree (PR greater than 0.20, all conducted), Mobitz I (PR lengthens until a beat drops; often benign), Mobitz II (beats drop without warning; often needs a pacemaker), third-degree (atria and ventricles completely independent; pacing).
Why
The two electrical treatments are separated by one idea: synchronized cardioversion waits for an R wave so it does not deliver energy during the vulnerable T wave, and it is used when there is an organized rhythm with a pulse. Defibrillation is unsynchronized because in VF there is no R wave to sync with. That is also why shocking a patient with a pulse without syncing can cause VF — the machine setting is the safety rationale.

The conduction system and its four properties.

SA node — near the surface of the right atrium at the junction with the superior vena cava. The primary pacemaker, firing spontaneously at 60–100/min, richly supplied by sympathetic (speeds) and parasympathetic (slows) nerves — so it has the greatest degree of automaticity.
The AV junction — a transitional cell zone, the AV node itself, and the bundle of His. The node lies beneath the right atrial endocardium between the tricuspid valve and the coronary sinus ostium. T (transitional) cells deliberately slow the impulse there, which appears as the PR segment.
Why the delay exists — it gives the atria time to contract and the ventricles time to fill. That contraction is the atrial kick, and it adds meaningful volume to cardiac output.
Below the node — the bundle of His continues through the interventricular septum, branching right and left, ending in the Purkinje fibers: an interweaving network on the endocardial surface of both ventricles from apex to base, partly penetrating the myocardium. These cells conduct rapidly, depolarizing the ventricles almost simultaneously.
Automaticity — the ability to generate an impulse spontaneously. Normally only the SA node does it, but with ischemia, electrolyte imbalance, hypoxia, drug toxicity, or infarction any cardiac cell can, and that is where dysrhythmias come from.
Excitability (depolarization) — the ability of nonpacemaker cells to respond to an impulse from pacemaker cells; normally negative cells develop a positive charge.
Conductivity — the ability to pass the stimulus from cell membrane to cell membrane, so cells depolarize in rapid succession. Disturbances occur when conduction is too fast, too slow, blocked entirely, or travels an abnormal pathway.
Contractility — the ability of atrial and ventricular muscle to shorten and push blood forward: the mechanical activity, as opposed to the electrical activity the strip shows.
Escape rates — a few ventricular nodal cells also show automaticity, producing ventricular beats or rhythms when the levels above them fail.
Why older adults are at higher risk — the SA node loses pacemaker cells and the bundle branch system loses fibers, so sinus node dysfunction is common and pacemakers are often needed. The most frequent dysrhythmias are PACs, PVCs, and atrial fibrillation — and they are more serious in older patients, because underlying heart disease plus age-reduced organ blood flow can tip into multisystem dysfunction.
Why
The four properties are not vocabulary — they are the four places a rhythm can break. A problem with automaticity gives you the wrong pacemaker (PVCs, ectopic beats); a problem with conductivity gives you a block; a problem with contractility gives you a rhythm on the monitor with no pulse at the wrist. That last one is why you always assess the patient before the strip.

Recording the rhythm — leads, paper, monitoring, artifact.

How a lead works — each lead has a positive and negative pole, and the imaginary line between them is the lead axis; the direction of current in the heart is the cardiac axis. Current toward the positive pole writes a positive deflection, away from it a negative deflection, and perpendicular to it a biphasic complex. The isoelectric line is the baseline, where no current is flowing.
The 12-lead ECG — six limb leads in the frontal plane and six chest (precordial) leads in the horizontal plane. Bipolar limb leads I, II, and III measure between two points with the right leg as ground: right arm always negative, left leg always positive, left arm either. Unipolar leads are aVR, aVL, aVF (a for augmented, third letter for positive electrode placement). An 18-lead. ECG adds six right-sided leads to view the right heart.
Placement is everything — serial ECGs are only comparable if placement is accurate and identical, which matters especially with chest deformities or large breasts; patients may be asked to move the breast, and a surgical marker can record the site. Remove electrodes afterward — skin breakdown occurs. Have the patient semi-reclined, still, breathing normally; any repetitive movement creates artifact and can produce a wrong interpretation.
Continuous monitoring — electrodes go on the trunk, not the limbs, because limb movement creates noise. Five-lead placement: right arm just below the right clavicle, left arm just below the left clavicle, right and left leg on the lowest palpable rib at the midclavicular lines, and a fifth for a chest lead.
Signal quality — clean the skin, clip hair if needed, make sure the site is dry, and use electrodes whose gel is moist and fresh. No lotion, no irritated skin, no scar tissue. Assistive personnel may apply electrodes, but the nurse selects the lead and verifies placement.
Hardwire versus telemetry — hardwired systems suit patients restricted to bed and chair; telemetry uses a battery-operated transmitter so ambulatory patients keep monitoring within a radius. Monitor technicians watch the bank, print strips, interpret, and report — but the nurse remains responsible for assessment and management. Full-disclosure monitors store rhythms so strips can be printed retrospectively.
Alarm safety is a National Patient Safety Goal — alarms only help if used well, and units full of competing alarms produce desensitization. Policy must cover clinically appropriate settings, when signals may be disabled, which parameters may be changed, and how alarms are monitored and answered. Think carefully before silencing an alarm or widening a parameter.
The paper — each small block is 1 mm; standard speed is 25 mm/sec, so a small block is 0.04 second and a large block (five small) is 0.20 second. Five large blocks make 1 second and 30 large blocks make 6 seconds; top-margin marks are usually 3 seconds apart.
Artifact — a wandering or fuzzy baseline from patient movement, loose or defective electrodes, improper grounding, or faulty cables. It can mimic lethal rhythms: toothbrushing can look like ventricular tachycardia and tapping an electrode like ventricular fibrillation. Assess the patient — never rely on the monitor alone.
The waveforms in detail — P wave: atrial depolarization, positive, negative, or biphasic depending on the lead, but consistent in shape when impulses come from the SA node; a changed shape means an ectopic focus fired. Q wave: first negative deflection, small normally, and when abnormally present it signals myocardial necrosis. R wave: first positive deflection. S wave: negative after the R. J point: where the QRS ends and the ST begins.
ST, T, and U — the ST segment is normally isoelectric and represents early repolarization; its length varies with rate, drugs, and electrolytes. The T wave is usually positive, rounded, slightly asymmetric, and becomes tall and peaked, inverted, or flat with ischemia, potassium or calcium imbalance, drugs, or autonomic effects. The U wave follows the T from slow Purkinje repolarization, is most common in V3, and an abnormal U wave suggests hypokalemia — do not mistake it for a P wave, and if in doubt request a potassium level.
The intervals — PR interval 0.12–0.20 s (five small blocks): atrial depolarization plus AV nodal delay plus travel to the Purkinje fibers. QRS duration normally under 0.11 s: depolarization of both ventricles. QT interval: total depolarization and repolarization, varying with age, sex, and rate — longer at slow rates, shorter at fast rates — and prolonged by drugs, electrolyte disturbance, or subarachnoid hemorrhage, which can produce torsades de pointes.
Why
Two of these details prevent real errors. First, artifact mimics death: a patient brushing their teeth can write what looks like VT, so the strip is never the whole assessment. Second, the U wave is a potassium message hiding in plain sight — mistake it for a P wave and you will misread the rhythm, recognize it and you catch a hypokalemia that is about to produce something worse.

The nine-step analysis, normal rhythms, and premature complexes.

1. Rate — the usual method is the 6-second strip: count QRS complexes in 6 seconds and multiply by 10 for the average rate. Monitors display a continuous rate, but confirm it against the patient; many factors distort what the monitor shows. Under 60 is bradycardia, over 100 tachycardia.
2–3. Regularity — march out the PP intervals with calipers for atrial regularity and the RR intervals for ventricular regularity, moving across the whole strip. Rhythms are regular, regularly irregular, occasionally irregular, or irregularly irregular. A variation of no more than three small blocks still counts as regular if all the complexes have the same shape — that small drift comes from intrathoracic pressure changes with breathing.
4. P waves — ask five questions: are they present? do they occur regularly? is there one P for each QRS? are they smooth, rounded, and upright? do they all look alike? More than one P shape, more P waves than QRS complexes, absent P waves, or P waves after the QRS each name a dysrhythmia.
5. PR interval — measure from the start of the P to the end of the PR segment; normally 0.12–0.20 s and constant. It cannot be measured at all if there are no P waves or if they come after the QRS.
6. QRS duration — from the start of the complex to the J point; normally 0.06–0.10 s and constant. Narrow (0.11 s or less) means supraventricular — the impulse was not formed in the ventricles. Wide (over 0.11 s) means ventricular origin, or supraventricular with aberrant conduction.
7. ST segment — displacement is significant at 1 mm or more, in two or more leads. Elevation suggests MI, pericarditis, or hyperkalemia; depression suggests hypokalemia, MI, or ventricular hypertrophy.
8–9. T wave and QT — note peaking or inversion; a normal QT should be no more than half the RR interval.
Normal sinus rhythm — atrial and ventricular rates 60–100, both rhythms regular, a consistent P before every QRS, PR 0.12–0.20 and constant, QRS 0.06–0.11 and constant.
Sinus arrhythmia — normal sinus rhythm except for irregularity: the rate rises slightly on inspiration and falls on exhalation, with the shortest and longest PP or RR intervals differing by more than 0.12 second. Common and normal in healthy adults — but digoxin and morphine can produce the same irregularity by enhancing vagal tone, unrelated to breathing.
Premature complexes — an irritable cell or group outside the SA node fires before the next sinus impulse, from atrial, junctional, or ventricular tissue. A pause follows before the next normal complex. Patients may feel nothing, or palpitations and a skipped beat; frequent ventricular ones can reduce cardiac output.
The repeating patterns — bigeminy is a repeating two-beat pattern (normal, premature, pause) so complexes come in pairs; trigeminy is two normals then a premature; quadrigeminy is three normals then a premature.
Why bradycardia can be good or bad — a slow rate lowers myocardial oxygen demand and the long diastole improves coronary perfusion time, both desirable. But if the rate is too slow to sustain output, coronary perfusion pressure falls — leading to ischemia or infarction, dysrhythmias, hypotension, and heart failure. The patient tolerates it well if the blood pressure is adequate; that is the deciding assessment.
Why tachycardia is dangerous — it shortens diastole and therefore coronary perfusion time, raises the work of the heart and its oxygen demand, and although output and pressure rise at first, continued speed cuts filling time and stroke volume until output, pressure, and coronary perfusion pressure all fall.
Cues of sustained brady- or tachydysrhythmia — chest discomfort or pain possibly radiating to jaw, back, or arm; restlessness, anxiety, nervousness, confusion; dizziness, syncope; palpitations; change in pulse strength, rate, and rhythm; pulse deficit; shortness of breath and tachypnea; crackles; orthopnea; S3 or S4; jugular venous distention; weakness and fatigue; pale or ash-gray cool skin and diaphoresis; nausea and vomiting; decreased urine output; delayed capillary refill; hypotension.
Causes to hold in mind — MI, electrolyte imbalance especially potassium and magnesium, hypoxia, drug toxicity, hypovolemia. Cocaine and illicit inhalants carry particular risk of fatal dysrhythmias; stress, fear, anxiety, and caffeine speed the rate; nicotine and alcohol excess can produce atrial fibrillation.
Why
Step 6 is the one that earns its keep. Width localizes the problem anatomically: narrow means the impulse used the fast conduction highway from above, wide means it either started in the ventricle or crawled an abnormal path muscle-to-muscle. Wide also means the atria did not contribute their kick — so QRS width predicts both where the rhythm came from and how much output it costs.

Sinus dysrhythmias and pacing.

Sinus tachycardia — the causes — sympathetic stimulation or vagal inhibition. Normal with activity (usually not above 160, rarely 180), but also anxiety, pain, stress, fever, anemia, hypoxemia, and hyperthyroidism; drugs including epinephrine, atropine, caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, cocaine, aminophylline, and thyroid medication. Often it is compensatory — for dehydration, hypovolemic shock, MI, infection, or heart failure.
What to assess in sinus tachycardia — fatigue, weakness, shortness of breath, orthopnea, falling oxygen saturation, rising pulse, falling blood pressure; restlessness and anxiety from reduced cerebral perfusion; decreased urine output from reduced renal perfusion; anginal pain and palpitations. The ECG may show T-wave inversion or ST elevation or depression from ischemia.
Treating it — treat the cause, not the number. Bed rest if the rate is causing hypotension or weakness, avoidance of caffeine, alcohol, and nicotine, and stress-management strategies.
Sinus bradycardia — the causes — excess vagal stimulation from carotid sinus massage, vomiting, suctioning, Valsalva maneuvers (bearing down, gagging), ocular pressure, or pain; also hypoxia, inferior wall MI, beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, digoxin, Lyme disease, electrolyte disturbance, neurologic disorders, and hypothyroidism.
The athlete's exception — well-conditioned athletes have a hypereffective heart: strong muscle delivers an adequate stroke volume at a low rate, so cardiac output is normal.
What to assess in bradycardia — syncope, dizziness and weakness, confusion, hypotension, diaphoresis, shortness of breath, chest pain. Review the record for drugs that slow SA or AV conduction.
Treating symptomatic bradycardia — IV atropine, IV fluids to increase intravascular volume, and oxygen if saturation is below 94% or the patient is short of air. Stop the suspected drug. For beta-blocker overdose, glucagon may raise rate and pressure. If the rate does not rise, prepare for transcutaneous or transvenous pacing; if the cause cannot be corrected, a permanent pacemaker follows.
Temporary pacing — a timed electrical stimulus when impulse initiation or conduction is defective, delivered to the right atrium, right ventricle (single-chamber), or both (dual-chamber). Used for symptomatic bradydysrhythmia unresponsive to atropine, and for asystole.
Transcutaneous versus transvenous — transcutaneous uses two large external electrodes and an external generator as an emergency bridge; it is painful and may require analgesia and sedation to tolerate. Transvenous threads a pacing wire to the right ventricle via the subclavian or femoral vein, attached to an external battery generator.
How the circuit works — impulses leave the negative terminal, travel the lead wire, depolarize the cardiac cells, and return through the other lead to the positive terminal. Output intensity is set in milliamperes.
Synchronous versus asynchronous — temporary pacing is usually synchronous (demand): sensitivity is set to detect the patient's own beats, so the pacemaker inhibits itself when the rate is above the set rate and paces when it falls below. Asynchronous means fixed-rate.
Capture — a pacing spike followed by evidence of depolarization (a P wave for atrial, a QRS for ventricular). Spike with no complex means loss of capture.
Permanent pacemakers — usually lithium-powered with an average life of about 10 years; the generator is replaced under local anesthesia. A biventricular pacemaker adds a lead to the left lateral wall through the coronary sinus, synchronizing the ventricles in moderate to severe heart failure. Leadless systems place a self-contained unit in the right ventricle via the femoral vein.
After insertion — monitor the rhythm for correct function; assess the site for bleeding, swelling, redness, tenderness, and infection; keep the dressing clean and dry; the patient should be afebrile with stable vital signs. Complications include pericardial effusion, tamponade, and diaphragmatic pacing — where the patient reports pain at the diaphragm and you can see muscle contractions synchronous with the heart rate.
Activity teaching — avoid raising the arm over the head or lifting more than 10 lb for 4 weeks because it can dislodge the lead, but usual arm movement is encouraged to prevent shoulder stiffness; avoid sudden jerky movements for 8 weeks while the device settles.
Living with it — take the pulse for a full minute at the same time daily and record it in a pacemaker diary; know the set rate and which changes to report; no pressure over the generator, no tight clothing or belts; baths and showers are fine; tell every provider about the device because tests such as MRI can damage it.
Electromagnetic precautions — avoid strong fields: magnets, telecommunications transmitters, radio, television, and radar towers, arc welding equipment, and leaning over running electrical or gasoline engines. Household appliances including microwaves, radios, and antennas are not hazards. Store antitheft devices can cause temporary malfunction — move away if symptoms start. Inform airport personnel and show the pacemaker identification card, since the metal triggers detectors, and wear a medical alert bracelet. Keep cell phones and magnetic-charging watches on the opposite side. If symptoms occur near any device, move 5–10 feet away and check the pulse.
What to report — difficulty breathing, dizziness, fainting, chest pain, weight gain, and prolonged hiccupping; any pulse rate below the set rate; fever, redness, swelling, or drainage at the site.
Why
Prolonged hiccupping sounds like a trivial complaint and is a device malfunction: a lead irritating the diaphragm paces it along with the heart. That is also why you look for twitching synchronous with the heart rate — the timing is what identifies the pacemaker as the cause rather than anything else.

Atrial dysrhythmias and atrial fibrillation in depth.

What shifts — in atrial dysrhythmias the impulse focus moves off the sinus node into atrial tissue, changing the direction of atrial depolarization and therefore the shape of the P wave.
Premature atrial complexes — an irritable atrial focus fires early; the premature P may be hidden in the preceding T wave, so examine T waves closely for a change in shape and compare them. Causes: stress, fatigue, anxiety, inflammation, infection, caffeine, nicotine, alcohol, and drugs (epinephrine, sympathomimetics, amphetamines, digoxin, anesthetics), plus ischemia, hypermetabolic states, electrolyte imbalance, and atrial stretch from heart failure, valvular disease, or pulmonary hypertension with cor pulmonale. Usually no treatment beyond the cause, but frequent PACs can precede worse atrial tachydysrhythmias.
Supraventricular tachycardia — rapid atrial stimulation at 100–280/min; P waves often invisible because they sit in the preceding T wave. It occurs in healthy young people, especially women, and is usually caused by a reentry mechanism circulating one impulse repeatedly. Paroxysmal SVT starts and stops abruptly, often triggered by a PAC.
SVT cues and consequences — palpitations, chest pain, weakness, fatigue, shortness of breath, nervousness, anxiety, hypotension, syncope. Sustained rapid rates can produce angina, heart failure, and cardiogenic shock.
SVT treatment ladder — if it occurs in a healthy person and self-terminates, remove the cause. Otherwise: vagal maneuvers, then adenosine, then AV nodal blockers (beta or calcium channel blockers), then synchronized cardioversion if perfusion is severely impaired. The preferred long-term treatment for recurrent SVT is radiofrequency catheter ablation after an electrophysiology study.
Vagal maneuvers, carefully — carotid sinus massage is performed by the provider over one carotid for a few seconds with the head turned slightly away, with a strip recorded before, during, and after, and vital signs and consciousness assessed afterward. Complications include bradydysrhythmias, asystole, ventricular fibrillation, and cerebral damage, so it is not commonly done and a defibrillator must be immediately available. The Valsalva version has the patient bear down as if straining at stool. Results are often temporary and can cause rebound tachycardia or severe bradycardia.
Atrial fibrillation — the mechanism — multiple rapid impulses from many atrial foci depolarize the atria chaotically at 350–600/min, with a ventricular response usually 120–200. No clear P waves, no atrial contraction, loss of atrial kick, irregular ventricular response. The atria merely quiver. Output falls by as much as 20–30%.
Scale and risk — the most common dysrhythmia in the developed world, projected to affect 12.1 million people in the United States by 2030, with incidence rising with age. It is associated with atrial fibrosis and muscle loss from hypertension, heart failure, and coronary artery disease; in patients without those, genetic mutations have been identified. Risk factors: hypertension, previous ischemic stroke or TIA, coronary heart disease, diabetes, heart failure, obesity, hyperthyroidism, chronic kidney disease, excess alcohol, and mitral valve disease.
The five categories — paroxysmal (converts back within 7 days), persistent (longer than 7 days), long-standing persistent (sustained over 12 months), permanent (a decision is made not to restore sinus rhythm), and nonvalvular (no mitral valve disease or repair). Definitive diagnosis is a 12-lead ECG.
The two priority problems — potential for embolus formation from the irregular rhythm, and potential for heart failure from the altered conduction pattern.
Both directions of embolism — thrombi in the right atrium travel through the right ventricle to the lungs, so watch for shortness of breath, chest pain, and hypotension and call the Rapid Response Team. Thrombi from the left side cause embolic stroke. Patients with valvular disease are also at risk for VTE — report leg pain and swelling and anticipate ultrasonography and systemic anticoagulation.
Rate control — calcium channel blockers such as diltiazem, amiodarone for difficult cases, beta blockers such as metoprolol and esmolol, and digoxin for patients with heart failure and AF — useful for rate but it does not convert the rhythm.
Rhythm control — flecainide, dofetilide, propafenone, and ibutilide, usually started in the acute care setting because of the risk of QT prolongation and bradycardia, with continuous monitoring and frequent 12-lead ECGs. Amiodarone does not require an acute stay. Dronedarone maintains sinus rhythm after cardioversion but is contraindicated in heart failure and in permanent AF, where it raises the risk of stroke, MI, and cardiovascular death. In permanent AF, rhythm-control drugs should not be used.
Anticoagulation — warfarin needs INR monitoring because of unpredictable response and food-drug interactions: avoid high-vitamin-K foods, and avoid ginger, ginseng, goldenseal, Ginkgo biloba, and St. John's wort. DOACs (dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban) reach a steady state and need no monitoring — and PT and INR are not accurate predictors of bleeding on them. Reversal: idarucizumab for dabigatran, andexanet alfa for rivaroxaban and apixaban — used only for life-threatening bleeding, because they sharply raise clot and stroke risk.
Health equity — Black patients are significantly less likely to receive DOACs, a disparity tied to access rather than need. Teach every patient the importance of the prescribed anticoagulant and confirm they can actually obtain it after discharge.
Electrical cardioversion — a synchronized countershock to stop the reentry circuit and let the sinus node resume control. If AF has lasted more than 48 hours, anticoagulate for at least 3 weeks or until INR is 2–3 first; if onset is unknown, a TEE checks the left atrium for clot. Consent, a short-acting anesthetic for sedation, one electrode left of the precordium and one right of the sternum below the clavicle, defibrillator in synchronized mode (a dot or line marks each QRS), usually starting at 120–200 joules biphasic.
Cardioversion safety — turn oxygen off and move it away from the patient (fire risk) and shout "CLEAR" before delivering the shock. Afterward: maintain the airway, give oxygen, assess vital signs and consciousness, give antidysrhythmics as prescribed, monitor for dysrhythmias, assess for chest burns from the electrodes, provide emotional support, and document. If the patient deteriorates into VF, check that the synchronizer is off so defibrillation can be delivered.
Left atrial appendage closure — for patients at high stroke risk who cannot take long-term anticoagulation. The LAA is the most common clot site in nonvalvular AF; the Watchman device is inserted percutaneously via the femoral vein through a transseptal puncture. Aspirin and warfarin follow, with a repeat TEE at about 45 days to check for leaks — if none, warfarin stops and antiplatelet therapy continues.
Radiofrequency catheter ablation — electrophysiologic mapping locates the irritable focus, then radiofrequency waves destroy it. In AF, pulmonary vein isolation creates scar that blocks the abnormal pathway. Ablation near the AV node or His bundle can damage normal conduction and require a permanent pacemaker; AV nodal ablation for rapid AF deliberately disconnects atria from ventricles and always requires one. Ablation should not be done if long-term anticoagulation is contraindicated.
The maze procedure — open-chest surgery, often with CABG, placing a maze of sutures in the atrial myocardium, pulmonary artery, and sometimes superior vena cava so electrical circuits cannot sustain AF, letting sinus impulses depolarize the atria and preserve the atrial kick. It is being replaced by a catheter maze, dragging a heated ablating catheter to create lines of block — fewer complications, less pain, faster recovery.
Why
The 48-hour rule is the most consequential number here. Within about two days of onset the atria have not had time to build organized clot, so cardioversion is relatively safe; past that, restoring a coordinated contraction can launch whatever has formed — which is why the treatment for the rhythm waits three weeks for the anticoagulant, and why an unknown onset buys a TEE instead of a shock.

Antidysrhythmic drug classes — Vaughn-Williams.

The overriding caution — the classification groups drugs by their effect on the cardiac action potential, classes I–IV. Most antidysrhythmics are proarrhythmic: they can worsen existing dysrhythmias and create new ones, so every one requires careful monitoring.
Class I — sodium channel blockers: membrane-stabilizing agents that decrease automaticity, in three subgroups — IA quinidine, IB lidocaine, IC flecainide and propafenone. Monitor blood pressure and rate for hypotension and bradycardia, watch for new or worse dysrhythmias, watch for CNS effects (dizziness, anxiety, ataxia, insomnia, confusion, seizures, GI distress), and watch for signs of heart failure, which many class I agents can cause.
Class II — beta blockers: propranolol, acebutolol, esmolol, sotalol. They compete for beta-adrenergic receptors, lowering rate and conduction velocity. Bradycardia and lower blood pressure are expected effects; assess for wheezing or shortness of breath from beta-2 bronchospasm, and for insomnia, fatigue, and dizziness. Sotalol is both class II and class III, because it also blocks potassium channels and prolongs the QT.
Class III — potassium channel blockers: each delays repolarization and prolongs the QT interval. Amiodarone works for atrial and ventricular dysrhythmias; monitor the rhythm continuously during infusion for bradycardia and AV block; approval is limited to life-threatening dysrhythmias because of lung damage and visual impairment, and corneal microdeposits occur in most patients without usually affecting vision.
The other class III agents — dronedarone (AF and flutter): take with meals, avoid grapefruit juice, report signs of heart failure since it is contraindicated there. Ibutilide: stop the infusion as soon as the dysrhythmia terminates or if VT appears, and correct potassium and magnesium before and during use. Dofetilide: change positions slowly, orthostatic hypotension is common. Bretylium (life-threatening ventricular dysrhythmias): monitor temperature for hyperthermia and blood pressure for hypotension.
Class IV — calcium channel blockers: only verapamil and diltiazem are approved for dysrhythmias. They slow calcium entry during depolarization, depressing SA and AV nodal automaticity, lowering rate and prolonging AV nodal refractory period. Bradycardia and hypotension are common; teach slow position changes on oral therapy until tolerance develops, and teach patients to report dyspnea, orthopnea, distended neck veins, or extremity swelling — heart failure can develop and require a dose change.
Unclassified — digoxin: used for AF and flutter. Assess the apical rate before giving it; a decreased rate is the expected response.
Unclassified — adenosine: for paroxysmal SVT. Give rapidly over several seconds followed by a normal saline bolus. Have emergency equipment at hand — a short period of asystole is common, and bradycardia and hypotension may occur. Facial flushing, shortness of breath, and chest pain are common side effects, along with nausea and vomiting.
Teaching that applies to all of them — generic and trade names, purpose, dose, side effects, and a written dosage schedule accounting for every drug the patient takes; report dizziness, nausea, vomiting, chest discomfort, or shortness of breath; never stop the drug abruptly unless instructed; take the pulse and report significant changes; keep follow-up appointments; and include family in the teaching.
Why
One line in this table reframes the whole chapter: most antidysrhythmics are proarrhythmic. The drug that fixes the rhythm can also be the reason the next one appears — which is why these patients stay on monitors, why potassium and magnesium are corrected before agents like ibutilide, and why "the rhythm changed after we treated it" is a finding to report rather than a success.

The five-step analysis.

Rate (6-second strip &times; 10, or 1500 &divide; small boxes), regularity of PP and RR intervals, P waves (present? regular? one per QRS? upright and identical?), PR interval (0.12–0.20 s and constant), and QRS duration — narrow means supraventricular, wide means ventricular or aberrant. Then ST segment, T wave, and QT (no more than half the RR).

E — Evaluate.

Ectopy decreases and disappears on the monitor; rate and rhythm controlled; potassium and magnesium in range; a toxic drug level falling. MAP and urine output recover, extremities warm, mentation clears. Anticoagulation therapeutic without bleeding. A device captures and senses reliably, and the patient demonstrates a full-minute pulse check and states their call parameters.
Unmet looks like: PVCs progressing to runs, any VT, a widening QRS or lengthening QT, new hypotension or altered consciousness, a platelet drop of 50% on heparin, pacer spikes without capture, or twitching synchronous with the heart rate. At that point the plan stops being a plan and becomes ACLS or a call to the provider.
Why
The monitor offers continuous evaluation data, which most nursing problems do not — and it can still mislead in both directions. The discipline is the same at both ends: confirm asystole in two leads, and confirm a reassuring rhythm against a patient you have actually touched.

On the exam: 6 questions — heart failure.
Heart failure, valve disease, cardiomyopathy, inflammation of the heart layers. Every one is a pump problem, and the cues are simply the answer to one question: which side failed, and where does the blood back up? Left backs up into the lungs; right backs up into the body.

Heart failure — the two sides, and the compensation that makes it worse.

Left-sided failure leads to lungs. Dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, crackles, cough with frothy pink-tinged sputum, S3 gallop, fatigue, confusion, oliguria by day and nocturia at night. Systolic (reduced EF — cannot contract) versus diastolic (preserved EF — cannot relax and fill).
Right-sided failure leads to body. Jugular venous distention, dependent pitting edema, weight gain, hepatomegaly and right upper quadrant discomfort, ascites, anorexia and nausea from gut congestion. Most commonly caused by left-sided failure; also by pulmonary disease (cor pulmonale).
Compensation — sympathetic activation raises rate and constricts vessels; the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system retains sodium and water; the ventricle dilates and hypertrophies. All three raise workload and oxygen demand, which is why compensation eventually accelerates the failure.
Key numbers — ejection fraction (normal 55–70%), BNP (rises with ventricular stretch), and daily weight: a gain of 2–3 lb in 24 hours or 5 lb in a week is fluid, and it is the teaching point that keeps patients out of the hospital.
Why
Weight is the most sensitive fluid measurement there is: a liter of retained fluid weighs a kilogram, and it appears on the scale days before it appears as crackles or edema. That is why "weigh yourself every morning, same time, same clothes, after voiding, before breakfast" is the single highest-value piece of heart failure teaching — it detects the problem while it is still fixable with a phone call.

Types, staging, and the compensatory cascade.

Three major types — left-sided, right-sided, and high-output failure. The ventricles are two separate pumping systems, so one can fail alone for a time, but most heart failure begins in the left ventricle and progresses to both.
Left-sided causes — hypertension, coronary artery disease, and valvular disease. It produces poor tissue perfusion from low output plus pulmonary congestion from raised pulmonary vessel pressure. Formerly called congestive heart failure, though not every case involves fluid accumulation.
Systolic failure (HFrEF) — the heart cannot contract forcefully enough to eject adequate blood. Preload rises as contractility falls and afterload rises with peripheral resistance. Ejection fraction drops from a normal 50–70% to below 40% with ventricular dilation. Sometimes called forward failure. An EF below 30% makes the patient a candidate for an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator because of sudden death risk.
Diastolic failure (HFpEF) — the left ventricle cannot relax adequately, so stiffening prevents filling. EF is above 40%, but more pressure is needed to move the same volume. It accounts for 20–40% of all heart failure, mostly in older adults and in women with chronic hypertension and undetected coronary disease. Cues and management resemble systolic failure.
Right-sided failure — caused by left ventricular failure, right ventricular MI, cardiomyopathies, pulmonic valve disease, or pulmonary hypertension; the right ventricle cannot empty, so volume and pressure build in the venous system. In the absence of left-sided failure it is usually from COPD, pulmonary hypertension, or ARDS.
High-output failure — output is normal or above normal, unlike the low-output states. Caused by increased metabolic need or hyperkinetic conditions: septicemia, high fever, anemia, hyperthyroidism. Less common than the others.
ACC/AHA stages against NYHA classes — A: at high risk, no symptoms or structural disease (NYHA I). B: structural abnormality or remodeling without symptoms (NYHA I). C: current or prior symptoms (NYHA II–III). D: refractory end-stage disease with symptoms that interfere with daily life and recurrent hospitalizations despite optimized therapy (NYHA IV). The Killip system stages by hemodynamic ability.
Compensation 1 — sympathetic stimulation, the most immediate mechanism, triggered by tissue hypoxia. Beta-adrenergic stimulation raises rate; alpha-adrenergic vasoconstriction raises pressure. But too fast a rate limits diastolic filling and output starts to fall, while oxygen demand climbs — and in a poorly perfused heart, failure worsens.
Compensation 2 — Starling stretch. Sympathetic stimulation increases venous return, stretching the fibers and dilating the ventricle, which contracts more forcefully. Past a critical point, more volume and stretch reduce the force of contraction. Arterial vasoconstriction maintains pressure but raises afterload — the major determinant of myocardial oxygen requirement.
Compensation 3 — RAAS activation from reduced renal flow. Angiotensin II intensifies vasoconstriction, aldosterone retains sodium and water, and both preload and afterload rise. Angiotensin II also drives ventricular remodeling and progressive myocyte contractile dysfunction.
Compensation 4 — other chemical responses. After MI, injured muscle triggers an immune response releasing TNF and interleukins 1 and 6, especially in left-sided failure, contributing to remodeling. Natriuretic peptides promote vasodilation and diuresis; BNP is released by stretching ventricles and rises with age, generally higher in healthy women. Low output also reduces cerebral perfusion, so the posterior pituitary secretes ADH — vasoconstriction and fluid retention, worsening failure. Stretched endothelial cells release endothelin, a potent vasoconstrictor raising resistance and pressure.
Compensation 5 — myocardial hypertrophy. Walls thicken for more muscle mass and more forceful contraction — but muscle can hypertrophy faster than collateral circulation can supply it, so a hypertrophied heart is often slightly oxygen deprived.
Causes and risk factors — most often systemic hypertension, then MI, then structural change such as pulmonic or aortic stenosis. Also cardiac infections and inflammations, cardiomyopathy, congenital defects, coronary artery disease, diabetes, dysrhythmias, family history, hyperkinetic conditions, obesity, severe lung disease, sleep apnea, tobacco, substance misuse, and valvular disease.
Scale — over 6 million people in the United States have heart failure, projected to exceed 8 million by 2030. It is the most common reason for hospital admission in people over 65. Black adults carry increased risk, attributed to higher rates of modifiable risk factors.
A drug trap in older adults — long-term NSAIDs for chronic pain cause fluid and sodium retention, may cause peripheral vasoconstriction, and increase the toxicity of diuretics and ACE inhibitors — a common reason a stable patient decompensates.
Why
Every compensatory mechanism in this list buys output now and costs the heart later, which is the single idea that makes heart failure pharmacology coherent. Faster rate, more stretch, more vasoconstriction, thicker muscle — all of them raise myocardial oxygen consumption. Symptoms appear at the moment demand outruns the reserve, so the drugs that help are the ones that undo compensation: slow the rate, drop the preload, drop the afterload, block the remodeling.

Assessing heart failure — both sides, and the tests.

History — ask about hypertension, angina, MI, rheumatic heart disease, valvular disorders, endocarditis, and pericarditis; then their perception of activity tolerance, breathing pattern, sleeping pattern, urinary pattern, fluid status, and what they understand about the disease. Prevention is the most effective treatment, which starts with identifying stage A patients who have risk but no symptoms.
Measuring activity tolerance concretely — can they do normal ADLs or climb stairs without fatigue or dyspnea? Can they do simultaneous arm and leg work, such as walking while carrying groceries, which can place an unacceptable demand on a failing heart? Ask for their most strenuous activity in the past week — patients unconsciously limit themselves and do not realize how much.
The cough — as output falls, hydrostatic pressure builds in the pulmonary veins and fills alveoli. Early failure gives an irritating, nocturnal, usually nonproductive cough. Frothy pink-tinged sputum means life-threatening pulmonary edema.
Left ventricular failure — low output side: fatigue, weakness, oliguria during the day with nocturia at night, angina, confusion and restlessness, dizziness, tachycardia and palpitations, pale or ash-gray skin, weak peripheral pulses, cool extremities.
Left ventricular failure — congestion side: hacking cough worse at night, dyspnea and breathlessness, crackles or wheezes, frothy pink-tinged sputum, tachypnea, and an S3 or S4 summation gallop. Respiratory rate typically exceeds 20.
The pulse — may be tachycardic or alternate in strength (pulsus alternans). Take the apical pulse for a full minute; PACs, PVCs, and atrial fibrillation are common, and a suddenly irregular rhythm can further compromise output.
S3 is the early sign — an early diastolic filling sound indicating raised left ventricular pressure, often the first sign of heart failure. S4 is not a sign of failure but reflects decreased ventricular compliance. Increased heart size displaces the apical impulse to the left.
The crackles that matter — late inspiratory, fine, profuse, repeating breath to breath and not diminishing with coughing, noted first at the bases and spreading upward as failure worsens. Wheezes indicate bronchial narrowing from engorged pulmonary vessels. Document precise location and whether wheezes are inspiratory, expiratory, or both.
Right ventricular failure — systemic congestion: jugular distention, enlarged liver and spleen, anorexia and nausea, dependent edema of legs and sacrum, distended abdomen, swollen hands and fingers, polyuria at night, weight gain, and blood pressure that is either raised (excess volume) or lowered (failure).
Abdominal findings — hepatomegaly, hepatojugular reflux, and ascites, which can exceed 10 L. Pressure on the stomach and intestines produces early satiety and malnutrition. Measure abdominal girth. Nausea and anorexia come directly from liver engorgement.
Edema is unreliable; weight is not — an adult may retain 4–7 L (10–15 lb) before pitting edema appears. Weight is the most reliable indicator of fluid gain and loss: same time each morning, same scale. Patients notice tighter shoes, sock indentations, and rings that no longer fit.
Thirst as a finding — patients with excess aldosterone may drink 3–5 L daily because of sodium retention, so take a careful nutrition history covering salt use, food types, and total daily fluid.
Psychosocial — chronic heart failure is slow and debilitating; anxiety and frustration are common and dyspnea amplifies anxiety. These patients are at high risk for depression, particularly older patients readmitted for an acute episode, and depression can appear months after diagnosis. Hope is a major indicator of well-being — hopeful patients feel better and stay socially involved, so ask about daily activities and contact with significant people.
Labs — electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium, chloride) regularly, because imbalance comes from the disease and from diuretics. Poor perfusion raises BUN and creatinine and lowers creatinine clearance. Hemoglobin and hematocrit identify anemia as a cause; hematocrit may read low from hemodilution in fluid excess.
BNP — used for diagnosis, particularly diastolic failure, in patients with acute dyspnea. An absence of BNP elevation with the history and exam rules heart failure out and points to primary lung dysfunction. Trends guide ambulatory treatment.
Urinalysis — may show proteinuria and high specific gravity. Microalbuminuria is an early indicator of reduced cardiac compliance and appears before BNP rises — an early warning detector well before symptoms.
Thyroid — check T4 and TSH in patients over 65, in atrial fibrillation, or with evidence of thyroid disease: heart failure can be caused or aggravated by either hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism.
ABGs — usually hypoxemia, because oxygen does not diffuse through fluid-filled alveoli. Respiratory alkalosis from hyperventilation, respiratory acidosis from CO&#8322; retention, and metabolic acidosis indicating lactic acid accumulation.
Imaging — chest x-ray shows cardiomegaly from hypertrophy or dilation, with pleural effusions generally reflecting biventricular failure. Echocardiography is the best diagnostic tool, showing valvular change, pericardial effusion, chamber enlargement, hypertrophy, and ejection fraction. Radionuclide studies and MUGA scans report ejection fraction and velocity.
ECG and hemodynamics — the ECG may show hypertrophy, dysrhythmias, and ischemia or infarction but is not helpful in determining the presence or extent of failure. Invasive monitoring assesses function and volume directly: right atrial pressure is normal or elevated in left-sided failure and elevated in right-sided failure, while PAP and PAOP are elevated in left-sided failure.
The priority problems — 1. decreased gas exchange from ventilation/perfusion imbalance; 2. potential for decreased perfusion from inadequate cardiac output; 3. potential for pulmonary edema from left-sided failure.
Improving gas exchange — monitor respiratory rate, rhythm, and quality every 1–4 hours and auscultate breath sounds every 4–8 hours. Supplemental oxygen within the prescribed range to keep saturation at or above 90%. For dyspnea, high-Fowler position with pillows under each arm to maximize chest expansion; reposition and do coughing and deep-breathing exercises every 2 hours to prevent atelectasis.
Why
The instruction that edema is an unreliable sign and weight is the reliable one is the practical heart of this chapter. Ten to fifteen pounds of fluid can be aboard before a thumbprint stays in the ankle — so a patient whose legs look fine may already be litres overloaded, and a scale used the same way every morning detects what inspection cannot.

Improving contractility, and the newer agents.

Digoxin's four benefits — increased contractility, reduced heart rate, slowed conduction through the AV node, and inhibition of sympathetic activity while enhancing parasympathetic activity. It reduces exacerbations and hospitalizations when added to ACE inhibitors or ARBs, beta blockers, and diuretics.
Why it is second-line — it may increase mortality through toxicity, especially in older adults. Absorption from the GI tract is erratic and many drugs interfere — notably antacids. Elimination is primarily renal, so older patients, those with impaired renal function, and those with low lean body mass need lower doses.
Toxicity and potassium — toxic levels or hypokalemia increase automaticity and produce ectopic beats, most often PVCs. A falling potassium makes the patient more sensitive to the drug, so a diuretic can create the toxicity the digoxin then expresses.
Recognizing toxicity — the signs are vague and nonspecific: anorexia, fatigue, blurred vision, and changes in mental status, especially in older adults. Nearly any dysrhythmia can appear, but PVCs are most common. Early signs are bradycardia, heart block, and loss of the P wave. Monitor apical rate and rhythm, and serum digoxin and potassium levels.
What to report — a new irregular rhythm in a previously regular patient, or a newly regular rhythm in a previously irregular one. Also be alert that any drug increasing cardiac workload raises oxygen demand — the patient may develop angina in response to digoxin.
The heart rate target — current evidence identifies a rate above 70 beats/min as a risk factor for increased mortality with negative clinical effects, because a faster rate shortens diastole, reduces coronary perfusion, raises oxygen requirement, drives noradrenaline synthesis, and promotes adverse remodeling.
Digoxin teaching — same time daily; do not stop unless told; do not take with antacids or laxatives; take the pulse before each dose and report a change in rate or rhythm, or increasing fatigue, muscle weakness, confusion, or loss of appetite. A missed dose may be delayed a few hours, but if remembered the next day, take only the usual daily dose. Keep laboratory appointments for potassium and digoxin levels.
IV inotropes for acute failure — dobutamine, a beta-adrenergic agonist, for short-term treatment of acute episodes, improving contractility, output, and myocardial and systemic perfusion. Milrinone, a more potent vasodilator/inotrope, is a phosphodiesterase inhibitor: it raises cyclic AMP, which enhances calcium entry into myocardial cells and increases contractile function. Both are given intravenously.
Beta blockers in heart failure — prolonged sympathetic stimulation and catecholamine exposure worsens cardiac function, and beta blockade reverses it, improving morbidity, mortality, and quality of life. Approved agents: carvedilol, extended-release metoprolol succinate, and bisoprolol — and do not confuse metoprolol tartrate with metoprolol succinate.
How they are started — slowly, with an extremely low first dose, monitored in hospital or the office for bradycardia or hypotension. Never stop abruptly — that risks clinical decompensation. Teach daily weights and immediate reporting of worsening failure; the dose is increased gradually if failure worsens.
What good beta blockade looks like — evaluate at least weekly for blood pressure, pulse, activity tolerance, and orthopnea. A modest pressure drop is acceptable if the patient stays asymptomatic and can stand without dizziness. Resting heart rate should stay between 55 and 60 and rise slightly with exercise. Most patients with mild to moderate failure show improved ejection fraction, fewer admissions, and better symptoms — but the benefit appears over a long period, not immediately.
Aldosterone antagonists — spironolactone or eplerenone, added when patients remain symptomatic on an ACE inhibitor and a beta blocker. Potassium-sparing, so they reduce dysrhythmia risk from hypokalemia, and they block aldosterone so less sodium and water is retained. Monitor for hyperkalemia and renal failure and expect to stop them if potassium or creatinine rise; every-other-day dosing is an option for those at risk of hyperkalemia.
Ivabradine — an HCN channel blocker that slows the rate by inhibiting a specific channel in the sinus node, reducing hospitalization risk. Used when the EF is under 35%, the patient is in sinus rhythm with a resting rate of 70 or more, and is on the maximally tolerated beta blocker dose or cannot take one. Side effects: bradycardia, hypertension, atrial fibrillation. Take with meals; teach radial pulse checks and reporting a low or irregular rate.
SGLT2 inhibitors — canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin. Developed for type 2 diabetes, but they significantly reduce hospitalizations and mortality and improve renal function in heart failure, and are now recommended whether or not the patient has diabetes. Watch for hypovolemia — hypotension, syncope, dehydration — and genitourinary fungal infection, particularly in women and with uncontrolled hyperglycemia.
Diastolic failure is the hard case — drug therapy has not been as effective. Calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors, and beta blockers are used with varying success.
Potassium monitoring long term — check serum potassium and renal function at least every few months on diuretics plus an ACE inhibitor, ARB, or sacubitril/valsartan. Loop diuretics deplete potassium; the RAAS blockers and potassium-sparing diuretics retain it. If potassium falls below 4 mEq/L, expect supplements or the addition of spironolactone or eplerenone, and teach potassium-rich foods. Commercial salt substitutes contain potassium, so renal status and potassium must be evaluated before using them — suggest lemon, spices, and herbs instead.
Why
Digoxin is the drug where the assessment is the safety mechanism. Its therapeutic window is narrow, its early toxicity looks like ordinary tiredness and poor appetite, and a diuretic sitting beside it on the same medication list quietly lowers the potassium that decides how much drug the heart binds. That is why a full-minute apical pulse and a potassium level are not routine tasks here — they are the two things standing between a therapeutic dose and a lethal one.

Devices, surgery, pulmonary edema, and living with it.

CPAP — treats obstructive sleep apnea in heart failure and also improves output and ejection fraction by decreasing afterload, preload, blood pressure, and dysrhythmias. Sleep apnea correlates directly with coronary artery disease because of diminished oxygen supply during apneic episodes.
Cardiac resynchronization therapy — biventricular pacing, alone or combined with an ICD, producing more synchronous ventricular contraction to improve EF, output, and mean arterial pressure. Indicated for class III or IV failure, EF under 35%, and a left bundle branch block.
Cardiac contractility modulation — for patients who do not meet CRT criteria. A pacemaker-like generator under the chest wall skin with leads in the right ventricle delivers current that increases contractility without increasing oxygen demand. Risks resemble pacemaker insertion, and the device must be recharged weekly. CRT remains preferred where the patient is eligible.
CardioMEMS — a quarter-sized permanent sensor implanted in the pulmonary artery during right heart catheterization. The patient lies on a special antenna pillow for a daily pulmonary artery pressure reading transmitted to the provider. It detects rising pressures — fluid retention — before symptoms appear, so therapy can be adjusted and readmission prevented.
Gene therapy — investigational, for end-stage failure in patients who are not transplant candidates: growth factor injections into the left ventricle, possibly improving exercise tolerance and regrowing cardiac cells.
Heart transplantation — the most definitive option for refractory end-stage failure, limited by donor availability and by the comorbidities these patients carry.
Health equity in transplant — Black and Hispanic adults are disproportionately affected by heart failure, yet transplant rates in these populations are declining, and the gap between transplant rates and mortality is greatest in Black patients. Achieving real equity requires deliberate intention, beginning with recognizing implicit bias and including diverse populations in policy development.
Ventricular assist devices — a mechanical pump implanted to work alongside the patient's own heart, left or right depending on the failure. Used as a bridge to transplant or as destination therapy for life. Not candidates: end-stage kidney disease, severe chronic lung disease, clotting disorders, and infections unresponsive to antibiotics. Complications: bleeding, infective endocarditis, ventricular dysrhythmias, and stroke. Once implanted the device flows continuously, creating a constant hum on auscultation, and the patient needs full-time support with extensive family training.
Left ventricular reconstruction — an alternative to transplant. In endoventricular circular patch cardioplasty the surgeon removes portions of the septum and left ventricular wall and grafts a circular patch, restoring a more normal ventricular shape to improve EF and output. Long-term survival benefit is not established.
Pulmonary edema — the mechanism: the left ventricle fails to eject enough blood, pulmonary pressure rises, and fluid leaks across the pulmonary capillaries into the airways and tissues. Causes include severe failure with fluid overload, acute MI, mitral valve disease, and dysrhythmias.
Pulmonary edema — the cues: crackles, dyspnea at rest, disorientation or acute confusion (an early symptom especially in older adults), tachycardia, hypertension or hypotension, reduced urine output, cough with frothy pink-tinged sputum, PVCs and other dysrhythmias, anxiety, restlessness, lethargy. Document the precise level of the crackles, since fluid climbs from the bases upward as it worsens. Patients are typically extremely anxious, tachycardic, and struggling for air, with cold clammy or cyanotic skin.
Pulmonary edema — the actions: if not hypotensive, sit them high-Fowler with the legs down to reduce venous return. The priority action is oxygen — 5–12 L/min by simple mask or 6–10 L/min by nonrebreather (up to 100%) — titrated to keep saturation above 90%. If distress persists, escalate to CPAP or BiPAP, and intubation with mechanical ventilation for some.
Pulmonary edema — the drugs: with systolic pressure above 100, sublingual nitroglycerin every 5 minutes for three doses while establishing IV access. Then rapid-acting diuretics — furosemide IV push over 1–2 minutes to avoid ototoxicity, or bumetanide the same way or as a continuous infusion for consistent removal over 24 hours. Vital signs every 30–60 minutes. If pressure is adequate, IV morphine reduces preload, anxiety, and the work of breathing — monitor respiratory rate and pressure closely.
Ultrafiltration — for severe fluid overload with renal dysfunction or diuretic resistance. It decreases cardiac filling pressures and pulmonary arterial pressure, increases cardiac index, and reduces norepinephrine, renin, and aldosterone.
The six heart failure core measures — a beta blocker prescribed at discharge; follow-up within 7 days; care record transmitted to the next level of care within 7 days; documented advance care planning discussion; advance directives in the record; and a discharge follow-up evaluation of status and adherence within 72 hours by phone, office visit, or home visit.
What actually reduces readmission — early and intensive nursing care (at least one visit on the day of discharge and three in the first post-hospital week) and provider follow-up within 1 week. Give the appointment date, time, and location verbally and in writing. Inability to get help with food shopping or obtaining medications is a major contributor to readmission, so involve the case manager. The teach-back method is associated with a lower 30-day readmission rate.
MAWDS self-management — Medications: take as prescribed, do not run out, know purpose and side effects, avoid NSAIDs. Activity: stay as active as possible without overdoing it, know your limits, be able to carry on a conversation while exercising. Weight: same time, same scale, every day. Diet: 2–3 g sodium and about 2 L fluid as prescribed. Symptoms: report anything new or worsening immediately.
Exercise, concretely — refer to cardiac rehabilitation (reimbursed, though patients may wait 6 weeks). Patients with persistent crackles and uncontrolled edema begin only after stabilizing. Start at 200–400 feet per day, walking at least three times a week and increasing slowly over months. Chest pain or severe dyspnea while exercising, or fatigue the next day, means advancing too quickly. Keep a diary of time, duration, heart rate, and symptoms.
What to report immediately — rapid weight gain (5 lb in a week or 2–3 lb in 24 hours), decreased exercise tolerance lasting 2–3 days, cold symptoms such as a cough lasting more than 3–5 days, excessive night waking to urinate, dyspnea or angina at rest or worsening angina, and increased swelling of feet, ankles, or hands.
Practical drug teaching — take diuretics in the morning to avoid nocturnal voiding; confirm the patient has a scale and can use it; some patients use a sliding scale to adjust the daily diuretic dose by weight, much as a patient with diabetes adjusts insulin by glucose. On ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or sacubitril/valsartan, move slowly when changing position and report dizziness, light-headedness, and cough.
Advance directives — heart failure is chronic, progressive, and debilitating, and the only potential cure is transplant. About 50% of heart failure deaths are sudden, many with no warning or worsening of symptoms, and most occur at home. Establish the patient's wishes early; if resuscitation is desired, make sure the family can activate EMS and perform CPR, and if it is not, plan together how the family will respond. Palliative care supports quality of life and symptom management as the disease declines, with hospice appropriate near the end of life.
Why
Notice that a cough lasting more than three to five days is on the list of things to report. It sounds like a cold and it is often the lungs filling — the same nocturnal, hacking, nonproductive cough that marks early left-sided failure. That is the recurring shape of heart failure teaching: the symptoms that matter most are the ones patients have a harmless explanation for, which is why the specific numbers and the specific days are what get written down and handed over.

Numbers and labs.

Daily weight on the same scale before breakfast — the most reliable fluid measure, since 4–7 L (10–15 lb) can be retained before pitting edema appears. Ejection fraction, BNP (absence of elevation helps rule heart failure out), electrolytes, BUN and creatinine, hemoglobin and hematocrit (may read low from hemodilution), microalbuminuria as an early marker, T4 and TSH over 65 or in AF, ABGs, chest x-ray, and echocardiography as the best diagnostic tool.

D — Diagnose & prioritize.

Cues cluster: orthopnea + PND + crackles + S3 + weight gain + edema + JVD = decompensated heart failure with fluid overload — then look for the precipitant, which is usually behavioral.
1. Decreased gas exchange related to pulmonary congestion and ventilation/perfusion imbalance — evidenced by hypoxemia, crackles, and tachypnea.
2. Excess fluid volume related to reduced output and sodium retention — evidenced by rapid weight gain, edema, and JVD.
3. Decreased cardiac output related to impaired contractility, altered filling, or an irregular rhythm.
4. Potential for pulmonary edema related to left-sided failure.
5. Activity intolerance related to reduced oxygen delivery.
6. Need for health teaching related to medication adherence, sodium restriction, and daily weight self-monitoring.
Why
Gas exchange outranks fluid volume even though the fluid caused it, because the lungs are where this kills tonight. And the teaching diagnosis is not padding here — missing the diuretic and eating high-sodium food are the two most common precipitants of readmission, which makes teaching a treatment rather than a discharge task.

Collaborative.

Cardiology; dietitian for practical label reading; pharmacy and case management for drug cost and refill access; cardiac rehabilitation starting at 200–400 feet per day; home health with a visit on the day of discharge; palliative care, and advance directives — about half of heart failure deaths are sudden.


CHAPTER 30: VASCULAR CONDITIONS

On the exam: 8 questions — hypertension, PAD and DVT.
Arteries carry blood to tissue under pressure; veins carry it back against gravity using valves and muscle. So arterial disease starves tissue and hurts with activity, while venous disease congests tissue and aches with standing. Get that one contrast and the entire chapter sorts itself.

Arterial insufficiency (PAD) — claudication, pain worse with elevation and relieved by dangling, cool pale shiny hairless skin, thick nails, weak or absent pulses, rubor on dependency and pallor on elevation, ulcers on the toes and lateral malleolus that are round, deep, and very painful, and minimal edema..


VTE, DVT, and anticoagulation.

Virchow triad — stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability. Every risk factor you can name (surgery, immobility, cancer, pregnancy, estrogen, obesity, smoking, central lines, long flights) fits one of the three.
DVT cues — unilateral calf or thigh pain, swelling, warmth, redness, and a difference in leg circumference. Many are silent; the first sign can be the embolus.
Never massage a suspected DVT. Confirm with venous duplex ultrasound; D-dimer supports but does not diagnose.
Pulmonary embolism — sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, anxiety and a sense of doom, hypoxemia, sometimes hemoptysis. Action: sit the patient up, high-flow oxygen, rapid response, IV access, anticoagulation as ordered.
Prevention — early ambulation, leg exercises, sequential compression devices and graduated stockings, hydration, and prophylactic anticoagulation.
Heparin — IV or subcutaneous, monitored by aPTT (1.5–2.5 × control); antidote protamine sulfate. Watch for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia: a platelet drop of 50% or more, paradoxically with new clots — stop all heparin, including flushes.
Low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin) — subcutaneous into the abdomen, do not expel the air bubble, do not aspirate or massage, no routine monitoring.
Warfarin — monitored by INR (target 2–3); antidote vitamin K; takes days to reach effect, so it overlaps with heparin. Teach consistent (not zero) leafy greens, and check every new drug for interactions.
Direct oral anticoagulants (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran) — no routine monitoring; teach adherence and bleeding precautions.
Bleeding precautions — soft toothbrush, electric razor, no NSAIDs or aspirin unless prescribed, fall prevention, report black stools, blood in urine, bleeding gums, unusual bruising, or a severe headache.
Why
Anticoagulants do not dissolve clots — they stop the clot that exists from growing while the body breaks it down. That is why the patient still needs the leg still and monitored, and it is why the entire nursing role is watching a deliberately created bleeding risk: two numbers (aPTT, INR), two antidotes (protamine, vitamin K), and one set of precautions.

Hypertension, aneurysm, and acute occlusion.

Primary hypertension (about 90%) has no single cause; secondary comes from kidney disease, endocrine disorders, or drugs. Stage 1 is 130–139/80–89; stage 2 is ≥140/90.
Why it matters — it is symptomless until it damages the target organs: brain (stroke), heart (hypertrophy, failure, MI), kidneys (failure), eyes (retinopathy), and vessels (aneurysm, PAD).
Lifestyle first — DASH-style eating, sodium reduction, weight loss, activity, limited alcohol, no tobacco, stress management. Then thiazides, ACE inhibitors or ARBs, calcium channel blockers, and others.
Teaching that prevents readmission — take the drug even when you feel well, change position slowly, do not stop abruptly, monitor at home and keep a log, and report a blood pressure over the parameters you were given.
Hypertensive crisis — above 180/120 with organ damage: severe headache, blurred vision, chest pain, dyspnea, neurologic change. Lower the pressure gradually with IV agents in a monitored setting; dropping it too fast causes stroke.
Aortic aneurysm — a permanent dilation to at least twice normal diameter. Often silent; abdominal aneurysm may show a pulsating mass and a bruit (do not palpate deeply). Rupture presents as sudden severe tearing back, flank, or abdominal pain with hypotension — a surgical emergency. After open or endovascular repair: monitor blood pressure tightly, distal pulses, urine output, and for back pain suggesting a leak.
Acute arterial occlusion — the six Ps: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia (coolness). Keep the limb dependent and at rest, do not apply heat or cold, notify immediately — this is limb-threatening and time is tissue.
Why
Hypertension is treated aggressively precisely because the patient feels fine — the damage is silent and cumulative, which makes adherence the entire clinical problem. And the six Ps are worth memorizing as a sequence rather than a list: pain and pallor come first, but paralysis and paresthesia mean nerve tissue is dying, and that is the point at which the limb is being lost.

Hypertension — mechanisms, causes, and drug therapy.

The equation — arterial pressure is the product of cardiac output and peripheral vascular resistance, and output is stroke volume times rate. Anything raising PVR, rate, or stroke volume raises pressure; anything lowering them lowers pressure and can reduce tissue perfusion. PVR is controlled by the autonomic nervous system and circulating norepinephrine and epinephrine.
Four control systems — the arterial baroreceptor system, regulation of body fluid volume, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and vascular autoregulation.
Baroreceptors — in the carotid sinus, aorta, and left ventricular wall. They counteract rising pressure by vagally mediated cardiac slowing and vasodilation with decreased sympathetic tone. Why baroreceptor control fails in hypertension is unclear.
Fluid volume — excess sodium or water raises venous return and therefore output and pressure. With adequate kidneys, a rising pressure produces diuresis and the pressure falls; pathologic states change the pressure threshold at which kidneys excrete sodium and water.
RAAS — the kidney produces renin, which splits angiotensin I from angiotensinogen; a lung enzyme converts it to angiotensin II, a strong vasoconstrictor that also controls aldosterone release. Aldosterone reabsorbs sodium in the collecting tubules, inhibiting fluid loss and raising volume and pressure. Renin should fall when pressure is high — but in most people with essential hypertension it stays normal, and inappropriate renin secretion may raise PVR.
The categories — ACC/AHA: normal under 120 and under 80; elevated 120–129 and under 80; stage 1 130–139 or 80–89; stage 2 140 or higher or 90 or higher. JNC-8 uses prehypertension 120–139 or 80–89, stage 1 140–159 or 90–99, stage 2 above 160 or above 100, with a target under 150/90 at 60 and older and under 140/90 below 60. ACC/AHA recommends below 130/80 for everyone.
What sustained pressure does — medial hyperplasia (thickening) of the arterioles. As vessels thicken and perfusion falls, organs are damaged: MI, stroke, peripheral vascular disease, and kidney failure.
Primary hypertension risk factors — family history, Black race, hyperlipidemia, smoking, age over 60 or postmenopausal, excessive sodium, overweight or obesity, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol, and excessive continuous stress.
Secondary causes — kidney disease is one of the most common; renovascular hypertension comes from renal artery stenosis and often improves with angioplasty and stenting. Also primary aldosteronism, pheochromocytoma, Cushing syndrome, coarctation of the aorta, brain tumors, pregnancy, obstructive sleep apnea, and illicit drugs.
The adrenal three — primary aldosteronism (usually a benign adrenal cortex adenoma) causes hypertension and hypokalemia; pheochromocytoma arises in the adrenal medulla and secretes excess catecholamines, producing life-threatening pressure; Cushing syndrome comes from excess glucocorticoids, most often adrenocortical hyperplasia or adenoma.
Drug causes — estrogen, glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, sympathomimetics, cyclosporine, and erythropoietin. Estrogen-containing oral contraceptives are likely the most common cause of secondary hypertension in women. The drugs are discontinued to reverse it.
Scale and equity — an estimated 121.5 million US adults 20 and older have high blood pressure. Up to age 64 a higher percentage of men are affected; after 64, women. Prevalence in Black Americans is among the highest in the world and rising, with more severe hypertension developing earlier in life and much higher death rates from stroke, heart disease, and kidney disease — which is why guidelines carry population-specific recommendations.
Lifestyle foundation — weight reduction through reduced calories plus activity; the DASH diet (high in fruits, vegetables, low-fat dairy, with more potassium, calcium, magnesium, and fiber); sodium reduction with an optimal goal under 1500 mg/day; and increased activity including aerobic, resistance, and static isometric exercise. Plus smoking cessation, stress reduction, and alcohol no more than one drink daily for women and two for men.
Assessment — most patients have no symptoms; some have headaches, facial flushing, dizziness, or fainting. Take readings in both arms, two or more per visit, with an appropriate-size cuff, allowing for white-coat anxiety. For orthostatics: supine or sitting, then again at least 3 minutes after standing. A funduscopic exam of the retina is a reliable index of severity and prognosis.
Physical findings that name a secondary cause — abdominal bruits suggest renal artery stenosis; tachycardia, sweating, and pallor suggest pheochromocytoma; high pressure in the arms with normal or low pressure in the legs indicates coarctation of the aorta.
Diagnostics — no lab diagnoses essential hypertension, but tests find secondary causes: protein and red cells in urine with raised BUN and creatinine for kidney disease; creatinine clearance (normal 107–139 mL/min men, 87–107 women) for glomerular filtration; urinary catecholamines for pheochromocytoma; raised serum corticoids and urinary 17-ketosteroids for Cushing syndrome. Chest x-ray may show cardiomegaly; on ECG, left atrial and ventricular hypertrophy is the first sign of hypertensive heart disease.
The priority problems — need for health teaching about the management plan, and potential for decreased adherence from drug side effects and required lifestyle change.
The four first-line classes — thiazide-type diuretics, calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors, and ARBs. Using an ACE inhibitor, ARB, and/or renin inhibitor simultaneously is potentially harmful and is not recommended. Non-responders may add other diuretics, an aldosterone antagonist, a beta blocker, or a renin inhibitor. Once-daily dosing is best, especially for older adults, because more doses means less adherence — though many patients need two or more drugs.
Thiazides — inhibit sodium, chloride, and water reabsorption in the distal tubule while promoting potassium, bicarbonate, and magnesium excretion, but they decrease calcium excretion, which helps prevent kidney stones and bone loss. Low cost and high effectiveness make them the usual choice in uncomplicated hypertension. Teach men about decreased libido and sexual performance; use cautiously in diabetes (glucose control), gout (uric acid retention), and prior significant hyponatremia.
Loop and potassium-sparing — loop diuretics (furosemide, torsemide, bumetanide) act on the ascending loop of Henle and promote potassium excretion; they are not commonly used in older adults because dehydration and orthostatic hypotension raise fall risk, so families report dizziness, falls, or confusion promptly. Potassium-sparing agents (spironolactone, triamterene, amiloride) have limited pressure effect and are usually combined with a loop or thiazide to offset potassium loss — with hyperkalemia as the risk.
Potassium in both directions — hypokalemia is the most frequent side effect of thiazide and loop diuretics: monitor levels and assess for irregular pulse, dysrhythmias, and muscle weakness; teach high-potassium foods (bananas, potatoes, orange juice) and expect most patients to need a supplement. With potassium-sparing agents, watch for hyperkalemia, which causes the same weakness, irregular pulse, and dysrhythmias plus painful leg cramping. Check renal function before giving them — renal failure is a side effect — and watch for tinnitus and hearing loss with high doses, prolonged use, or rapid infusion. Take diuretics in the morning to prevent nocturia.
Calcium channel blockers — dihydropyridines (nifedipine, amlodipine) lower pressure by vasodilation; nondihydropyridines (verapamil, diltiazem) also act directly on the heart, blocking SA and AV conduction and lowering rate, so use cautiously in heart failure, bradycardia, or heart block. CCBs with thiazide diuretics are the first-line therapy for Black patients. Teach avoidance of grapefruit and grapefruit juice, which can enhance the drug to the point of kidney failure, heart failure, or GI bleeding. Clevidipine butyrate is IV only, by infusion pump, for hypertensive emergencies.
ACE inhibitors — the "-pril" drugs block conversion of angiotensin I to II, decrease sodium and water retention, and lower PVR. The common side effect is a nagging dry cough, reported promptly — and the drug is discontinued if it develops. Get out of bed slowly with the first dose because of severe hypotension; later doses are usually less severe. Notify the provider for continued dizziness or a systolic drop of more than 20 mm Hg, and hold if systolic is below 100. Assess for hyperkalemia, since they reduce potassium excretion. Older patients are at greatest risk of orthostatic hypotension.
ARBs — the "-sartan" drugs competitively block angiotensin II at receptor sites in vascular smooth muscle and adrenal tissue without inhibiting ACE. Excellent for patients with the ACE cough, and they need no initial dose adjustment for older adults or renal impairment. Like ACE inhibitors, less effective in Black patients unless combined with a diuretic, beta blocker, or CCB. Teach avoidance of high-potassium foods because of hyperkalemia risk.
Beta blockers — the "-olol" drugs, cardioselective (atenolol, bisoprolol, metoprolol) or noncardioselective, lowering rate and myocardial contractility and suppressing renin. Do not give if the rate is under 50–60; hold for systolic under 90–100 and contact the provider. Teach about fatigue, weakness, depression, and sexual dysfunction. In diabetes, they may mask the usual signs of hypoglycemia and inhibit gluconeogenesis. They are often the drug of choice when the patient has heart disease, since the heart is the most common target of end-organ damage. Never stop abruptly — taper over about 2 weeks, because abrupt cessation can cause angina or MI.
Hypertensive crisis — above 180/120, progressing rapidly, an emergency. The patient may be asymptomatic or have morning headaches, blurred vision, anxiety, nosebleeds, and dyspnea, and without prompt intervention may develop kidney failure, left ventricular failure, or stroke.
Managing crisis — critical care admission with IV therapy: nitroprusside, clevidipine, fenoldopam, or labetalol. Reduce systolic by no more than 25% in the first hour, then if stable to about 160/100 over the next 2–6 hours, then cautiously toward normal over 24–48 hours. Gradual reduction is essential — rapid reduction causes cerebral ischemia, MI, and renal failure. Give oxygen and monitor saturation; once stable, switch to oral agents.
Adherence is the clinical problem — patients need these drugs for life, and they stop because they feel no symptoms and do have side effects. Collaborate with the pharmacist so the regimen fits the patient's actual lifestyle and schedule; nonadherence leads directly to target organ damage and crisis.
Why
Hypertension is treated hard precisely because it feels like nothing. The damage is silent medial thickening of arterioles, and the patient's own experience gives them no reason to keep taking a drug that causes a cough, or fatigue, or sexual dysfunction. That is why the two priority problems in this chapter are teaching and adherence rather than anything physiologic — the pathophysiology is already solved; the behavior is not.

Arteriosclerosis, atherosclerosis, and lipid management.

The distinction — arteriosclerosis is thickening or hardening of the arterial wall, often with aging. Atherosclerosis is a type of it involving plaque formation within the wall, and is the leading risk factor for cardiovascular disease. It affects larger arteries: coronary beds, aorta, carotid and vertebral, renal, iliac, and femoral.
The sequence — vessel damage causes inflammation in response to cellular injury; a fatty streak appears on the intimal surface; collagen migrates over it through cellular proliferation to form a fibrous plaque that protrudes into the lumen. Finally the plaque becomes calcified, hemorrhagic, ulcerated, or thrombosed and affects all layers of the vessel.
Stable versus unstable plaque — when stable plaque ruptures, thrombosis and constriction obstruct the lumen, causing inadequate perfusion distally. Unstable plaque is prone to rupture, is often clinically silent until it ruptures, and causes more severe damage: exposed underlying tissue triggers platelet adhesion and rapid thrombus formation that can suddenly block the vessel, producing ischemia and infarction.
What injures the intima — high LDL-C and low HDL-C chemically injure the wall; so do circulating toxins in renal failure and carbon monoxide from cigarette smoking. The wall is weakened by aging itself and by diseases such as hypertension.
Genetics and diabetes — some patients have familial hyperlipidemia with a liver making excessive cholesterol and other fats; others with hereditary atherosclerosis have normal cholesterol, for reasons not understood. Severe diabetes at any age frequently produces premature and severe atherosclerosis from microvascular damage, because diabetes raises LDL-C and triglycerides and hyperglycemia itself damages arteries.
Risk factors — low HDL-C, high LDL-C, raised triglycerides, genetic predisposition, diabetes, obesity, hypertension, sedentary lifestyle, smoking, stress, Black or Hispanic race and ethnicity, older age, and a diet high in saturated and trans fats, cholesterol, sodium, and sugar. An estimated 126.9 million US adults have one or more cardiovascular diseases, and small plaques are almost always present in the arteries of young adults.
Assessment — a complete cardiovascular assessment with pressure in both arms. Palpate pulses at all major sites and note differences, and palpate each carotid separately to avoid blocking blood flow to the brain. Check temperature differences and capillary filling: prolonged filling (over 3 seconds in young to middle-aged adults, over 5 seconds in older adults) suggests poor circulation, though it is not the most reliable indicator.
The bruit, again — a turbulent swishing sound from blood forcing through a narrowed artery, common in carotid, aortic, femoral, and popliteal arteries. Abnormal, but it does not indicate severity.
The critical finding — a decrease in intensity or complete loss of a pulse may indicate arterial occlusion in the area that artery supplies. If not palpable, confirm with Doppler, then report pulselessness immediately and document for emergency management.
Screening — low-risk adults 20 and older should have total cholesterol checked at least every 4–6 years, more often with multiple risk factors or age over 40.
Nutrition — a pattern emphasizing vegetables, fruits, and whole grains; low-fat dairy, skinless poultry, fish, legumes, nontropical oils such as canola, and nuts; limited sweets, sugar-sweetened beverages, and red meat; 5–6% of calories from saturated fat with trans fats limited; plant stanols and sterols about 2 g/day plus soluble fiber; and fiber increased to 30 g daily. Meats and eggs are mostly saturated fat and high in cholesterol.
Physical activity — aerobic activity three or four times a week, averaging 40 minutes per session at moderate to vigorous intensity, to reduce LDL-C.
Statins — HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors (lovastatin, atorvastatin, simvastatin, fluvastatin, rosuvastatin, pravastatin, pitavastatin) reduce cholesterol synthesis in the liver and increase LDL-C clearance, lowering both LDL-C and triglycerides. Contraindicated in active liver disease and pregnancy; they can cause muscle myopathies and marked decreases in liver function, and interact with warfarin, cyclosporine, and selected antibiotics. Discontinue for muscle cramping or elevated liver enzymes. Other effects: abdominal bloating, flatulence, diarrhea, constipation.
The grapefruit mechanism — especially with atorvastatin, lovastatin, and simvastatin, limit grapefruit to no more than half a grapefruit or 8 ounces of juice daily. Grapefruit's furanocoumarins inactivate the enzyme CYP3A4 that metabolizes many drugs, so too much statin stays in the bloodstream, causing adverse interactions or muscle injury.
Other agents — ezetimibe inhibits cholesterol absorption in the small intestine, reducing delivery to the liver and increasing clearance; combinations include Vytorin (ezetimibe and simvastatin, working two ways), Caduet (amlodipine and atorvastatin), and Pravigard (aspirin and pravastatin) — and combining drugs can improve adherence. PCSK9 inhibitors (alirocumab, evolocumab) are given subcutaneously monthly or bimonthly for familial hypercholesterolemia or when other therapy fails on maximally tolerated statins.
Complementary agents — niacin may lower LDL-C and VLDL and raise HDL-C, but tolerability is poor: low doses are used because of flushing and a warm sensation, and higher doses raise hepatic enzymes; it is useful with other drugs in statin-intolerant patients. Lovaza (omega-3 ethyl esters) is approved as a diet adjunct for triglycerides above 500 mg/dL.
The framing that matters — guidelines support regular lab monitoring, but care is based on patient risk and modifying that risk, not on the lab value alone. Statins are an adjunct to lifestyle modification, not a substitute for it.
Why
The most dangerous plaque is the one that has never caused a symptom. Stable plaque narrows the lumen gradually and announces itself as claudication or angina; unstable plaque may barely narrow anything, stay silent, then rupture and thrombose a vessel in minutes. That is why risk factor modification is aimed at the whole arterial tree rather than at the lesion someone found — the lesion that kills is usually not the one being watched.

Peripheral arterial disease — stages, ulcers, and management.

Terms — peripheral vascular disease covers any disorder changing flow through peripheral arteries or veins; a diagnosis of PVD generally implies arterial disease (PAD). Some patients have both. PAD in the legs is also called lower extremity arterial disease.
The mechanism — PAD is a result of systemic atherosclerosis: partial or total occlusion decreases perfusion, and tissue below the obstruction cannot survive without adequate oxygen and nutrients. Patients with PAD have increased risk of chronic angina, MI, and stroke.
Inflow versus outflow — the dividing line is the inguinal ligament. Inflow obstructions involve the distal aorta and common, internal, and external iliac arteries (above the ligament). Outflow obstructions involve the femoral, popliteal, and tibial arteries. Gradual inflow occlusions may not cause significant tissue damage; gradual outflow occlusions typically do.
Scale and equity — about 8.5 million US adults aged 40 and older have PAD, and Black Americans are affected at twice the rate of other ethnicities — a significant health disparity.
Stage I — asymptomatic: no claudication; a bruit or aneurysm may be present; pedal pulses decreased or absent.
Stage II — claudication: muscle pain, cramping, or burning with exercise, relieved by rest, and reproducible — the same distance produces the same pain, and the distance shortens as disease progresses.
Stage III — rest pain: pain at rest that commonly wakes the patient at night, described as numbness, burning, or a toothache-type pain, usually in the toes, arch, forefoot, or heel and rarely the calf or ankle, relieved by placing the extremity dependent. Rest pain signals advanced disease that may cost the limb.
Stage IV — necrosis and gangrene: ulcers and blackened tissue on toes, forefoot, and heel, with a distinctive gangrenous odor.
Inflow disease pain map — discomfort in the lower back, buttocks, or thighs. Mild: after about two blocks, not severe but stops them, relieved by rest. Moderate: pain after one or two blocks, eased by rest most of the time. Severe: pain after less than one block, usually with rest pain.
Outflow disease pain map — burning or cramping in the calves, ankles, feet, and toes; instep or foot discomfort indicates obstruction below the popliteal artery. Mild: after about five blocks. Moderate: after about two blocks, with intermittent rest pain. Severe: less than half a block, hanging the feet off the bed at night, with more frequent rest pain than inflow disease.
Inspection findings — loss of hair on the lower calf, ankle, and foot; dry, scaly, dusky, pale, or mottled skin; thickened toenails. With severe disease the limb is cold and gray-blue or darkened, with pallor on elevation and dependent rubor when lowered. Prolonged disease produces muscle atrophy.
Assessing cyanosis across skin tones — in light pigmentation it appears bluish around the lips or eyes; in dark pigmentation as an ash gray or lightened color around the lips with a gray or slightly blue cast around the eyes. The soles of the feet and toenails are less pigmented, which can help detect duskiness in the lower extremities.
The most useful pulse — the posterior tibial is the most sensitive and specific indicator of arterial function, because the pedal pulse is not palpable in a small percentage of people. Compare strength bilaterally.
Arterial ulcers — history of claudication after one to two blocks, usually with rest pain, pain at the ulcer site, and two or three risk factors. Located at the ends of and between the toes, deep, with a pale bed, even edges, and little granulation tissue. Foot cool or cold, pulses decreased or absent, skin atrophy, hair loss, pallor on elevation, dependent rubor, possible gangrene. Treat the underlying cause by revascularization, prevent trauma and infection, and teach foot care.
Venous ulcers — a chronic nonhealing ulcer with no claudication or rest pain, moderate discomfort, and reports of ankle or leg swelling. Located in the ankle area with brown pigmentation, superficial with a pink bed, uneven edges, and granulation tissue present. Pulses present, no neurologic deficit, ankle discoloration and edema, full veins when the leg is slightly dependent, scarring from previous ulcers. Managed with long-term wound care (Unna boot, damp-to-dry dressings) and elevation.
Diabetic ulcers — diabetes with peripheral neuropathy and no claudication. Located on the plantar area, metatarsal heads, and pressure points, deep, pale, with even edges and little granulation. Pulses usually present, foot cool or warm, and the ulcer is painless. Management: rule out major arterial disease, control the diabetes, teach foot care, prevent infection.
Diagnostics — MRA with contrast is commonly used and is often the only test needed, with CTA as an alternative. Segmental Doppler pressures at thigh, calf, and ankle are inexpensive and noninvasive: normally leg pressures exceed arm pressures, and with arterial disease they are lower. In inflow disease, a thigh pressure 10–30 mm Hg below brachial suggests mild disease and over 40–50 mm Hg severe disease.
ABI, and its false reassurance — ankle pressure divided by brachial pressure; under 0.90 in either leg is diagnostic of PAD. Patients with diabetes have a falsely elevated ABI, so a normal number does not exclude disease in them.
Exercise tolerance testing — resting pulse volume recordings, then walking on a treadmill until symptoms appear or about 5 minutes. Normally the waveform increases with minimal ankle pressure drop; with arterial disease waveforms are dampened and ankle pressure falls 40–60 mm Hg for 20–30 seconds. Return to normal taking longer than 10 minutes suggests abnormal flow. Plethysmography graphs arterial flow, with waveforms decreased to flattened depending on the degree of occlusion.
Exercise as therapy — it builds collateral circulation: smaller vessels develop to compensate for the occluded one. Not for patients with severe rest pain, venous ulcers, or gangrene. Teach walking to the point of claudication, stopping to rest, then walking a little farther, with distance increasing over time as collaterals develop. It relieves symptoms but requires a motivated patient.
Positioning — teach patients to avoid raising the legs above heart level, because extreme elevation slows arterial flow to the feet. In severe cases patients may sleep with the affected leg hanging off the bed or sit upright in a chair for comfort. Avoid crossing the legs and avoid restrictive clothing, and inspect the feet daily for color change and other differences.
Promoting vasodilation — keep the limb and the home warm, with socks or insulated shoes at all times, and avoid cold, which causes vasoconstriction. Never apply direct heat with heating pads or very hot water — sensitivity is decreased and burns result. Emotional stress, caffeine, and nicotine also constrict vessels, and the vasoconstrictive effect of a single cigarette may last up to an hour, so complete abstinence is essential.
Drug therapy — antiplatelet agents: aspirin or clopidogrel for symptomatic PAD to reduce MI, stroke, and vascular death, sometimes as dual therapy, which is reasonable for limb-related events after lower extremity revascularization. Patients on clopidogrel should avoid grapefruit, which reduces its antiplatelet effect. Cilostazol, a phosphodiesterase inhibitor, improves symptoms and walking distance and can raise HDL-C, but causes headaches and GI upset, especially flatulence and diarrhea. Pentoxifylline was used historically — evidence shows minimal benefit and cilostazol is more effective.
The blood pressure balance — controlling hypertension improves perfusion by keeping pressure adequate to perfuse the periphery without constricting vessels. Note that beta blockers may cause or worsen claudication, so patients on them are monitored closely, and statins are used for high lipids.
Why
Two opposite instincts get corrected in this chapter. First, do not elevate the arterial leg — gravity is part of what is still delivering blood to that foot. Second, do not warm it with a heating pad, even though warmth is exactly what you want: the same disease that starves the tissue has also blunted its sensation, so the patient will not feel the burn until it is a wound that cannot heal.

Revascularization, graft care, and acute occlusion.

After a percutaneous endovascular intervention or atherectomy — the priority is bleeding at the arterial puncture site, sealed with a collagen plug. Watch for impending hypovolemic shock: falling pressure, rising pulse, decreasing urine output. Complications include hematoma, retroperitoneal bleeding, pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, nerve compression, and atheroembolism. Check the puncture site and distal pulses in both legs frequently. Most patients get heparin or clopidogrel before or during, and an antiplatelet drug afterward.
Who gets surgery — patients with severe rest pain, or claudication that interferes with work or threatens the limb. Arterial revascularization is the most common procedure.
Inflow versus outflow procedures — inflow bypasses occlusions above the superficial femoral arteries (aortoiliac, aortofemoral, axillofemoral); outflow bypasses at or below them (femoropopliteal, femorotibial). When both exist, the inflow procedure is done first. Inflow procedures are more successful with less reocclusion; outflow procedures relieve ischemic pain less reliably and reocclude more often.
Graft material — for outflow procedures the preferred graft is the patient's own saphenous vein, though it may be needed for a future coronary bypass; cephalic or basilic arm veins are alternatives, and synthetic grafts are used when no autogenous vein is available.
Anesthesia choice — epidural or spinal is preferred for older adults to reduce cardiopulmonary complications. The axillofemoral bypass tunnels graft material subcutaneously from beneath the clavicle to the groin without entering the thoracic or abdominal cavity, so it suits high-risk patients who cannot tolerate abdominal surgery.
After open aortoiliac or aortofemoral bypass — NPO for at least 1 day, because nausea and vomiting would raise intraabdominal pressure. Lower extremity bypasses not involving the aorta or abdominal wall may have clear liquids on the first postoperative day. Deep breathing every 1–2 hours with an incentive spirometer.
What is expected afterward — warmth, redness, and edema of the affected extremity are expected outcomes of increased arterial perfusion, not signs of trouble.
Hand-off as a safety measure — the operating suite or PACU nurse marks the site where the distal pulse is best palpated or heard by Doppler and communicates it in the hand-off report, so the receiving nurse checks the same spot.
Protecting graft patency — monitor blood pressure and notify the surgeon if it rises or falls beyond baseline; hypotension may mean hypovolemia, which raises clotting risk. Range of motion is limited with no bending of the hip and knee; open procedures may mean bed rest for 24 hours or longer to protect suture lines, while minimally invasive patients may walk and eat the same day with less pain and fewer complications.
Graft occlusion — a postoperative emergency within the first 24 hours. Severe continuous aching pain may be the first indicator. Many patients have a throbbing pain from increased flow, which is different from ischemic pain — so assess the type of pain. Occlusion pain may be masked by PCA, and some ischemic pain is not relieved by PCA at all.
Checking the graft — assess the extremity every 15 minutes for the first hour, then hourly for color, temperature, and pulse intensity, comparing with the other leg. Cold, pale, ashen, or cyanotic, or a decreased or absent pulse: contact the surgeon immediately.
Treating occlusion — emergency thrombectomy, sometimes at the bedside, with excellent results in prosthetic grafts but less success in autogenous vein grafts, which often need revision or replacement. Local intraarterial thrombolytic therapy with an agent such as TPA is an alternative when surgery carries high morbidity — then assess closely for bleeding.
Graft and wound infection — life-threatening. Use sterile technique for incisional care and watch for induration, erythema, tenderness, warmth, edema, or drainage, plus fever and leukocytosis; notify the surgeon promptly. Open bypass usually means 5–7 days in hospital; minimally invasive, 2–3.
Foot care teaching for peripheral vascular disease — wash with mild soap in room-temperature water; dry thoroughly, especially ankles and between the toes; wear comfortable well-fitting shoes and never go without shoes; keep toenails clean and cut straight across, having someone else do it if vision is poor; apply lubricating lotion to prevent dry cracked skin; never use a heating pad and avoid temperature extremes; avoid constricting garments and extended pressure such as leaning against something; inspect daily; and see the provider at the first sign of a sore.
Acute arterial occlusion — sudden and dramatic, unlike chronic PAD. An embolus is the most common cause, usually originating in the heart, and most patients have had an acute MI or atrial fibrillation within the previous weeks. More common in the legs than the arms.
Presentation — severe pain below the occlusion even at rest, with a cool or cold, pulseless, mottled extremity and possibly blackened or gangrenous toes. The six Ps: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, and poikilothermy (coolness).
Treatment — prompt unfractionated heparin to prevent further clot formation, then angiography; surgical thrombectomy or embolectomy under local anesthesia through an arteriotomy, retrieving the embolus by catheter, sometimes closing the artery with a patch graft.
After thrombectomy — observe color, temperature, and pulse every hour for the first 24 hours. Watch for new thrombi or emboli, especially pulmonary embolism: chest pain, dyspnea, and acute confusion in older adults — notify the provider or Rapid Response Team immediately. Comfort should improve markedly; only mild incisional pain remains.
Reperfusion injury and compartment syndrome — reperfusing the artery causes spasm and swelling of skeletal muscle. Compartment syndrome occurs when tissue pressure in a confined space rises and restricts blood flow, producing ischemia, tissue damage, and eventually death of tissue. Assess motor and sensory function and monitor for increasing pain, swelling, and tenseness — report immediately, because fasciotomy may be needed to save the limb.
Catheter-directed thrombolysis — systemic thrombolytic therapy for acute arterial occlusion has been disappointing because bleeding outweighs the benefit. Instead the catheter tip is embedded in the clot to deliver fibrinolytic directly, taking 24–36 hours. Monitor for bleeding and hemorrhagic stroke; maintaining a normal blood pressure is essential to prevent stroke. As the clot dissolves the patient typically has severe reperfusion pain requiring PCA.
Fibrinolytic safety — assess for bleeding, bruising, and hematoma. With any platelet inhibitor, monitor platelet counts at 3, 6, and 12 hours after starting the infusion or per protocol; if platelets fall below 100,000/mm&sup3; the infusion is readjusted or stopped. Notify the provider or Rapid Response Team immediately.
Why
The postoperative pain assessment here is unusually consequential. Both the success and the failure of the surgery hurt — restored flow throbs, and a clotted graft aches continuously — and a PCA pump will blunt one while hiding the other. So the question is never "is the patient in pain," it is what kind, and the answer is checked against a limb whose color, warmth, and pulse you are already tracking every fifteen minutes.

Aneurysms, dissection, and the other arterial problems.

Definition and shapes — a permanent localized dilation enlarging an artery to at least twice its normal diameter. Fusiform means diffuse dilation of the whole circumference; saccular means an outpouching of one portion. True aneurysms have a wall weakened by congenital or acquired problems; false ones follow injury or trauma to all three layers. Dissecting aneurysms form when blood accumulates within the arterial wall.
The mechanism — the media weakens, stretching the intima and outer layers. As the artery widens, wall tension rises, causing further widening — a self-accelerating cycle that raises rupture risk. Elevated blood pressure increases the rate of enlargement and the risk of early rupture.
Where they occur — most commonly the abdominal aorta, at points where the artery is not supported by skeletal muscle or along curves and flexion points. AAAs are most common, usually between the renal arteries and the aortic bifurcation, commonly asymptomatic, and frequently rupture. TAAs are less common and frequently misdiagnosed, usually found incidentally on imaging, developing between the origin of the left subclavian artery and the diaphragm.
Causes — atherosclerosis is the most common, with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and cigarette smoking contributing, plus age, gender, and family history.
AAA assessment — abdominal, flank, or back pain, usually steady with a gnawing quality, unaffected by movement, lasting hours or days. A pulsation may be visible in the upper abdomen slightly left of midline between the xiphoid and umbilicus; a detectable aneurysm is at least 5 cm. Auscultate for a bruit over the mass but do not palpate it — it may be tender and there is rupture risk.
Impending or actual rupture — severe pain of sudden onset in the back or lower abdomen, possibly radiating to groin, buttocks, or legs. A rupturing AAA is critically ill with hypovolemic shock: hypotension, diaphoresis, decreased consciousness, oliguria, loss of pulses distal to the rupture, and dysrhythmias. Retroperitoneal hemorrhage shows as flank hematomas; rupture into the abdominal cavity causes distention.
TAA assessment — back pain plus compression of adjacent structures: shortness of breath, hoarseness, difficulty swallowing. Rarely detectable on exam, though a mass may be visible above the suprasternal notch. Suspected rupture presents as sudden excruciating back or chest pain with hypovolemic shock.
Diagnosis — CT with contrast is the standard for size and location; ultrasonography is also used.
Nonsurgical management — monitor growth and keep the blood pressure normal to reduce rupture risk, treating hypertension to slow enlargement. Small or asymptomatic aneurysms need frequent ultrasound or CT, so emphasize keeping those appointments and teach which symptoms to report at once: abdominal fullness or pain, chest or back pain, shortness of breath, difficulty swallowing or hoarseness.
Surgical management — emergency surgery for rupture; elective surgery for smaller symptomatic aneurysms; nonsurgical monitoring for small asymptomatic ones until symptoms appear or they enlarge. Elective open resection carries a high mortality rate that markedly increases for emergency surgery.
Endovascular stent grafts — now the procedure of choice for almost all patients, elective or emergent. A flexible Dacron or PTFE graft is inserted through a femoral artery incision by catheter, advanced above the aneurysm away from the renal arteries, and fixed with a series of hooks; postoperative care resembles care after an arteriogram. Complications: conversion to open repair, bleeding, aneurysm rupture, peripheral embolization, misplacement of the graft, endoleak, and infection.
Discharge teaching after repair — activity restrictions, wound care, pain management; no lifting more than about 15–20 lb for 6–12 weeks; caution with pulling, pushing, or straining; no driving for several weeks. Stair climbing may be restricted at first, so a bedside commode may be needed if the bathroom is inaccessible. Check that the patient has transportation to monitoring appointments.
Peripheral aneurysms — femoral and popliteal aneurysms are uncommon but often accompany an aneurysm elsewhere. Detect a popliteal aneurysm as a pulsating mass in the popliteal space and a femoral one as a pulsatile mass over the femoral artery — do not palpate the mass, and evaluate both extremities since more than one may be present. There may be limb ischemia and pain from nerve compression. Surgery is recommended regardless of size because of thromboembolic risk: femoral aneurysms are removed and circulation restored with a graft, while most surgeons prefer to bypass rather than resect a popliteal aneurysm. Afterward, palpate pulses below the graft to assess patency and report sudden pain or discoloration immediately as possible graft occlusion.
Aortic dissection — more accurately a dissecting hematoma. A sudden tear in the aortic intima lets blood enter the wall; degeneration of the media may be the primary cause with hypertension an important contributor, and it is associated with Marfan syndrome and other connective tissue disorders. It peaks in the 50s and 60s and affects men more often. Any major artery arising from the aorta can lose its circulation, making it highly lethal.
Dissection presentation — pain described as sharp, tearing, ripping, stabbing that moves from its point of origin, felt in the anterior chest, back, neck, throat, jaw, or teeth, commonly at 10 out of 10. Also diaphoresis, nausea, vomiting, faintness, pallor, a rapid weak pulse, and apprehension. Blood pressure is usually elevated unless tamponade or rupture has occurred, then rapidly hypotensive. Decreased or absent peripheral pulses are common, as is aortic regurgitation with a musical murmur along the right sternal border, and neurologic deficits including altered consciousness, paraparesis, and stroke.
Confirming it — CTA is the test of choice if the patient is stable, but they are often too unstable to transport, so TEE at the bedside is used.
Emergency management — goals are comfort and reducing systolic pressure to 100–120 mm Hg. Two large-bore IVs for 0.9% sodium chloride and drugs, and an indwelling urinary catheter. Pain control plus an IV beta blocker such as esmolol to lower rate and pressure; if ineffective, nitroprusside or nicardipine. Distal dissections are managed medically; proximal dissections surgically, typically requiring cardiopulmonary bypass to remove the intimal tear and suture the dissected edges with a synthetic graft. Long-term target pressure is under 120/80 on beta blockers and calcium channel antagonists.
Buerger disease — claudication in the feet and lower extremities, worse at night, with ischemia and fibrosis of vessels, increased cold sensitivity, and ulceration and gangrene of the digits. Cause unknown but associated with smoking. Managed with vasodilators such as nifedipine, ulcer and gangrene care, and persistent pain management. Teach: stop smoking, avoid cold with gloves and warm clothing, use stress management, and avoid caffeine.
Raynaud phenomenon — a benign condition of vasospasm in arteries and arterioles of the extremities, especially the digits, triggered by cold or stress; cause unknown, more common in women. Calcium channel blockers such as amlodipine are used when nonpharmacologic measures fail. Teach: avoid vasoconstricting drugs including over-the-counter decongestants, stop smoking, avoid temperature extremes and keep hands, fingers, and toes warm, and avoid caffeine.
Subclavian steal — subclavian artery occlusion or stenosis causing arm ischemia and pain, with paresthesias and dizziness, a blood pressure difference between arms, and a subclavian bruit on the affected side. Surgery (endarterectomy, bypass, or dilation) for cyanosis or unrelenting pain; postoperatively check pulses and watch for ischemic change including severe pain or cyanosis.
Thoracic outlet syndrome — compression of the subclavian artery by a rib or muscle, usually with a rib abnormality or injury, causing neck, arm, and shoulder pain with numbness and possible cyanosis. Physical therapy to strengthen the muscles around the shoulder, avoiding aggravating positions, with surgery as a last resort for severe pain; neurovascular assessments throughout.
Why
Two instructions in this section are the same instruction: do not palpate the mass. A pulsatile abdominal mass and a pulsatile popliteal mass both invite the hands, and both are thin-walled vessels under pressure. Confirm with the stethoscope and with imaging — the exam maneuver that would satisfy your curiosity is the one that can end the patient.

Venous thromboembolism — risk scoring and prevention.

What veins need — to be patent, to have competent valves, and to have surrounding muscle beds pumping blood toward the heart. Three problems alter venous flow: thrombus formation (which can cause fatal pulmonary embolism), defective valves (venous insufficiency and varicose veins — not life-threatening but problematic), and failure of skeletal muscle contraction, which happens when weight bearing is limited or muscle tone decreases.
Terms — VTE covers both DVT and PE. A thrombus results from endothelial injury, venous stasis, or hypercoagulability — one element or all three — and is often associated with inflammation: immunity is altered, inflammation forms around the clot, the vein wall thickens, and embolization may follow. Thrombophlebitis is a thrombus with inflammation, which can occur in superficial veins but most often occurs in the deep veins of the legs.
Why DVT matters more than superficial phlebitis — it carries a far greater risk of pulmonary embolism, where a dislodged clot travels to the pulmonary artery. DVT develops most often in the legs but increasingly in the upper arms because of central venous devices.
Highest-incidence procedures — hip surgery, total knee replacement, and open prostate surgery. Other promoting conditions: ulcerative colitis, heart failure, cancer, oral contraceptives, and immobility — including prolonged bed rest and prolonged sitting on an airplane or at a computer. COVID-19 produces a hypercoagulable state with high DVT risk, and VTE incidence rose significantly during the pandemic, with critical care patients experiencing two to three times the rate of others. Phlebitis from IV therapy also predisposes to thrombosis.
Core measures and payment — The Joint Commission's VTE core measure set requires reporting of prophylaxis and management, including electronic measures for medical-surgical and intensive care units. If hospital-acquired VTE is not prevented or adequately managed, the hospital may not be paid for that care. Every inpatient must be assessed for VTE risk on admission.
The Padua Prediction Score — assesses risk, it does not diagnose. 3 points each for active cancer on chemotherapy, previous VTE excluding superficial thrombosis, reduced mobility, and a known thrombophilic condition. 2 points for trauma or surgery within a month. 1 point each for age 70 or older, cardiac or respiratory failure, acute MI or ischemic stroke, acute infection or rheumatologic disorder, BMI 30 or above, and ongoing hormonal treatment. A score of 4 or more means VTE is more likely.
The classic cues — calf or groin tenderness and pain with sudden unilateral leg swelling. Examine the painful area against the other limb, gently palpating for induration along the vessel and for warmth and edema; redness may be present. Many patients are asymptomatic.
Homans sign is not advised — calf pain on dorsiflexion appears in only a small percentage of patients with DVT and false positives are common, so it is an unreliable tool.
Diagnostics — the preferred test is venous duplex ultrasonography. Doppler flow studies are more sensitive for proximal than distal. DVT: normal veins create audible signals while thrombosed veins produce little or none, and accuracy depends on the operator's skill. If the test is negative but suspicion remains, a venogram may be needed. Magnetic resonance direct thrombus imaging is better than traditional venography for DVT in the inferior vena cava or pelvic veins.
D-dimer — a global marker of coagulation activation measuring fibrin degradation products from clot breakdown. Used when clinical signs are few, and it stratifies patients into a high-risk category for recurrence. A negative D-dimer can exclude DVT in a low-risk patient — it is an adjunct to noninvasive testing, not a diagnosis.
The priority problem — potential for injury from complications of VTE and of anticoagulation therapy: the disease and its treatment are both hazards.
The prevention bundle for moderate to high risk — patient education, leg exercises, early ambulation, adequate hydration, graduated compression stockings, intermittent pneumatic compression such as sequential compression devices, a venous plexus foot pump, and anticoagulant therapy.
The change in thinking about activity — supportive therapy traditionally meant bed rest and elevation, but research shows ambulation does not increase the risk of pulmonary embolus. The risk with more aggressive activity is unknown, so the accepted approach is a gradual increase in ambulation as tolerated — which also reduces the patient's fear that moving will dislodge the clot.
Ongoing measures — elevate the legs in bed and chair; wear knee- or thigh-high graduated compression stockings for an extended period in active and resolving DVT to prevent chronic venous insufficiency and reduce swelling, sized from the manufacturer's chart. Some providers prescribe intermittent or continuous warm moist soaks to promote circulation and reduce pain.
The absolute rule — do not massage the affected extremity, to keep the thrombus from dislodging and becoming an embolus. Monitor every patient for PE: shortness of breath, chest pain, and acute confusion in older adults. Emboli can also travel to the brain or heart, though less commonly.
Anticoagulants carry their own safety goal — they are the drugs of choice for actual DVT and for patients at risk, but they are known to cause medical complications and death, which is why The Joint Commission's National Patient Safety Goals include specific performance elements to reduce anticoagulation harm.
Why
Two long-standing habits get retired here, and both for the same reason: they generated false confidence. A Homans sign misses most DVTs and flags legs that do not have one, and strict bed rest was imposed for decades on a fear the evidence does not support. What replaced them is less satisfying and more useful — a risk score calculated on admission, a duplex ultrasound, and mechanical prophylaxis applied before anything has happened.

Risk and VTE screening.

Virchow triad — stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability — and every risk factor fits one. Score inpatients with the Padua Prediction Score on admission (4 or more means higher risk). Cues of DVT: unilateral calf or groin pain, swelling, warmth, redness, induration — but many are silent. Homans sign is not advised. Screen continuously for PE: sudden dyspnea, pleuritic pain, tachycardia, anxiety, and acute confusion in older adults.

Pressure and tests.

Blood pressure in both arms and a funduscopic exam for hypertension severity; look for abdominal bruits (renal artery stenosis), tachycardia with sweating and pallor (pheochromocytoma), and arm pressure higher than leg (coarctation). ABI: normal 1.00 or higher, under 0.9 diagnostic, under 0.5 severe — falsely elevated in diabetes. Then venous duplex, D-dimer, segmental pressures, MRA or CTA, and baseline CBC, platelets, PT/INR, aPTT, and creatinine before anticoagulation.

D — Diagnose & prioritize.

1. Potential for pulmonary embolism related to venous thromboembolism — the risk that kills in minutes, and often the first sign of a silent DVT.
2. Inadequate peripheral tissue perfusion related to arterial occlusive disease — evidenced by claudication or rest pain, a low ABI, absent pulses, and an arterial ulcer.
3. Potential for injury from complications of anticoagulation — the disease and its treatment are both hazards, including HIT.
4. Impaired tissue integrity related to venous congestion or arterial insufficiency.
5. Acute and chronic pain related to ischemia or venous congestion.
6. Need for health teaching and potential for decreased adherence related to lifelong antihypertensive therapy, anticoagulation, smoking cessation, and side-specific limb care.
Why
Adherence earns a place on the diagnosis list in this chapter rather than in the teaching notes, because hypertension is symptomless and its drugs are not — patients stop them because they feel nothing wrong and do feel the cough, the fatigue, or the sexual dysfunction.

Independent — venous/VTE.

Do not massage a suspected DVT. Elevate the affected leg in bed and chair; graduated compression stockings sized from the chart; gradual increase in ambulation as tolerated — evidence shows walking does not raise embolus risk. Measure calf circumference daily at a marked point. Prevention bundle: leg exercises, early ambulation, hydration, SCDs, and prophylactic anticoagulation.

On the exam: 20 questions — lab value interpretation including anemia types, sickle cell patient education, iron-deficiency, pernicious, folic acid and vitamin B12 anemia, and leukemia.
Three failures, in order: too few red cells (anemia), too few or too many white cells (leukemia, lymphoma, myeloma, neutropenia), and clotting gone wrong (thrombocytopenia, DIC). The nursing priorities repeat: oxygen delivery, infection prevention, and bleeding precautions.

The anemias — cause to cue to care.

Shared cues of any anemia — fatigue, pallor, dyspnea on exertion, tachycardia, dizziness, cold intolerance, headache, and in older adults confusion or a fall. Severity tracks how fast it developed, not just the number.
Iron deficiency — the most common. Causes: blood loss (menses, GI bleeding — always ask about stool color), poor intake, malabsorption. Cues add spoon-shaped nails, a smooth sore tongue, and pica. Teach oral iron: take on an empty stomach with vitamin C for absorption, expect dark stools, prevent constipation, use a straw for liquid forms, and do not take with dairy, antacids, or caffeine. IM/IV iron by Z-track when oral fails.
Vitamin B₁₂ deficiency and pernicious. anemia — B₁₂ needs intrinsic factor from the stomach to be absorbed, so gastric surgery or autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor causes it. Unique cues: neurologic — paresthesias, ataxia, poor proprioception, confusion — plus a beefy red sore tongue. Pernicious. anemia requires lifelong parenteral or intranasal B₁₂, because the problem is absorption, not intake.
Folic acid deficiency — same red cell picture as B₁₂ (large cells), no neurologic signs. Alcohol use, poor diet, pregnancy, malabsorption.
Aplastic anemia — marrow failure of all lines (pancytopenia). Infection and bleeding risk dominate; treatment is removing the cause, immunosuppression, transfusion, and stem cell transplant.
Hemolytic anemia — red cells destroyed early; jaundice, dark urine, splenomegaly, high reticulocytes and bilirubin.
Sickle cell disease — an inherited beta-chain mutation (HbS) makes cells sickle under stress: hypoxia, dehydration, infection, cold, acidosis, high altitude, extreme exertion. Sickled cells occlude vessels, causing pain crises and organ infarction. Crisis care: oxygen, aggressive hydration, opioid analgesia on a schedule (these patients are chronically undertreated), warmth, rest, and infection treatment. Prevention: hydroxyurea, vaccinations, folic acid, avoiding triggers.
Thalassemia — reduced or absent globin chain production; ranges from trait to transfusion dependence with iron overload requiring chelation.
Polycythemia vera — too many cells; thick sluggish blood, ruddy face, itching after a hot shower, headache, and a high clot risk. Treatment is therapeutic phlebotomy, hydration, and anticoagulation or myelosuppression.
Why
Two discriminators carry most anemia items. Neurologic symptoms separate B₁₂ from folate, because B₁₂ is required to maintain myelin — and that is why giving folate to a B₁₂-deficient patient fixes the blood count while the nerve damage silently progresses. And sickling is triggered by anything that lowers oxygen or concentrates the blood, which is why hydration and oxygen are the crisis interventions and why dehydration is the most preventable trigger.

White cell cancers and clotting failures.

Leukemia — uncontrolled production of immature white cells that crowd out normal marrow. The three consequences are the presentation: infection (no functional neutrophils), anemia (fatigue, pallor), and bleeding (low platelets — petechiae, gum bleeding, bruising), plus bone pain, fever, night sweats, and weight loss. Acute versus chronic, myeloid versus lymphoid. Diagnosis by bone marrow biopsy; treatment is induction chemotherapy, consolidation, and sometimes stem cell transplant.
Nursing priority in leukemia — infection prevention comes first: it is the leading cause of death. Protective precautions, no live plants or flowers, no raw foods per policy, meticulous hand and oral hygiene, no rectal temperatures or suppositories, and a fever of 100.4 °F is an emergency.
Lymphoma — Hodgkin (Reed-Sternberg cells, often a single painless enlarging node, highly curable) and non-Hodgkin (more varied, often widespread). "B symptoms": fever, drenching night sweats, and weight loss over 10%. Treated with chemotherapy and radiation; teach late effects and second-cancer surveillance.
Multiple myeloma — malignant plasma cells in the marrow producing useless antibody. The tetrad: bone pain and fractures, hypercalcemia, kidney failure, anemia. Care is hydration (to protect the kidneys and lower calcium), pain control, fracture precautions and safe handling, bisphosphonates, and infection prevention.
Neutropenia — ANC below 1000 is risk, below 500 is severe. The patient cannot mount normal cues, so fever may be the only sign. Cultures, then broad-spectrum antibiotics within the hour.
Thrombocytopenia — below 50,000 means bleeding with trauma; below 20,000 means spontaneous bleeding. Bleeding precautions, no IM injections or rectal procedures, soft toothbrush, electric razor, fall prevention, and watch for the worst outcome: intracranial bleeding (headache, vision change, altered consciousness).
Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) — widespread clotting consumes platelets and factors, so the patient clots and bleeds at the same time: oozing from every site plus organ ischemia. Labs: low platelets and fibrinogen, prolonged PT and aPTT, high D-dimer. Treat the underlying cause (sepsis, trauma, obstetric emergency, transfusion reaction), replace products, and support perfusion.
Transfusion nursing — two-nurse verification, 18–20 gauge access, normal saline only, stay for the first 15 minutes at a slow rate, full set of vitals before, at 15 minutes, and after, complete within 4 hours. Any reaction: stop, keep the line open with saline through new tubing, notify, return the bag.
Why
Every white-cell cancer produces the same three problems because the marrow is one shared factory: fill it with useless cells and you lose oxygen carriers, defenders, and sealants simultaneously. That is why the care plan barely changes between leukemia, aplastic anemia, and chemotherapy-induced marrow suppression — infection prevention, bleeding precautions, and activity pacing — and why the exam can ask about any of them with the same answers.

Sickle cell disease — the full picture.

The vasoocclusive event — clumped masses of sickled cells block flow, causing tissue hypoxia, which causes more sickling, more obstruction, and more ischemia. Hypoxia is the most common trigger; others are dehydration, high altitude, infection, smoking, stress, weather changes, strenuous exercise, and blood loss.
Why damage becomes permanent — cells usually return to normal shape once oxygen normalizes, but some hemoglobin stays twisted, reducing flexibility. Membranes are damaged over time until cells are permanently sickled, and HbS membranes are more fragile and break more easily. A sickle cell lives 10–20 days versus the normal 120, which is what produces the hemolytic anemia.
Crisis pattern — pain crises (vasoocclusive crises) have sudden onset and may come as often as every few weeks or as seldom as once a year. Many patients are in good health much of the time, with crises only in response to triggers.
Cumulative organ damage — small infarcts and scar tissue progressing to organ failure, most often in the spleen, liver, heart, kidney, brain, joints, bones, and retina. Average life expectancy is about 22 years less than adults without the disease.
Genetics — autosomal recessive, from a mutation in the hemoglobin gene alleles on chromosome 11 producing HbS instead of HbA. With two HbS alleles the patient usually has 80–100% HbS and severe symptoms even from mild triggers. Every child of a patient with SCD inherits at least sickle cell trait.
Sickle cell trait — one normal and one abnormal allele, so only about half the hemoglobin chains are abnormal. Carriers can pass it on and usually have only mild symptoms when precipitating conditions occur — and may not know they have it until an acute illness or until anesthesia is administered.
Who is affected — all races and ethnicities, particularly people whose ancestors came from sub-Saharan Africa, South America, the Caribbean, Central America, Saudi Arabia, India, Turkey, Greece, and Italy. About 100,000 people in the United States have SCD, occurring in 1 in 365 Black or African American births, with about 1 in 13 (8%) born with the trait.
History to take — previous crises and what preceded them, usual severity, and what the patient does to manage them. Recent contact with ill people and signs of infection. Then all activities and events in the past 24 hours: food and fluid intake, temperature extremes, drugs, exercise, trauma, stress, travel, alcohol, tobacco, other substances. Sleep and rest patterns, stair climbing, and any activity causing shortness of breath. Rate energy level 0 to 10, with 10 as total exhaustion.
Cardiovascular — the anemia creates risk for high-output heart failure: shortness of breath, fatigue, weakness, murmurs, an S3, and raised jugular pulsation or distention. Compare pulses, temperature, and capillary refill in all extremities — limbs distal to occlusion are cool with slow refill and reduced or absent pulses. Heart rate is usually rapid, pressure low to normal.
Acute chest syndrome — a serious acute lung injury with pulmonary infiltrates on x-ray, fever, chest pain, arm and/or leg pain, tachypnea, cough, and wheezing. It is the most common acute respiratory disorder in SCD — 50% of patients have at least one episode — and accounts for 25% of deaths, usually from respiratory failure and multiple organ dysfunction. In adults it usually develops after admission for something else, such as a vasoocclusive crisis, which can release fat emboli or marrow into venous circulation that travels to the lungs. Other triggers: infection, asthma exacerbation, hypoxemia, postoperative complications. Many patients also develop pulmonary hypertension and all are at risk for recurrent pneumonia.
Priapism — a prolonged, painful erection from excessive vascular engorgement of erectile tissue that can last hours, during which the patient usually cannot urinate. Report it to the provider immediately.
Skin — pallor or cyanosis from poor gas exchange, and jaundice from red cell destruction releasing bilirubin. Assess lips, tongue, nail beds, conjunctivae, palms, and soles for cyanosis, and the sclera closest to the cornea, palms, soles, hard palate, and mucous membranes for jaundice — at least every 4 hours for subtle changes. Many adults have leg ulcers on the outer sides and inner ankle or shin that become necrotic or infected and need debridement and antibiotics.
Abdomen, kidneys, joints, CNS — spleen and liver damage occurs early from repeated hypoxia, and in crisis abdominal pain is diffuse and steady, involving the back and legs, with a firm enlarged liver and spleen later. Almost one-third develop chronic kidney disease from anoxic nephron damage: protein in the urine, inability to concentrate urine, eventually little or no output. Joints may be necrotic from hypoxic episodes. Cognitive and behavioral changes are often the earliest sign of cerebral hypoxia — ask caregivers whether the current mental status is usual, and assess pronator drift, bilateral grasp strength, gait, and coordination; repeated infarcts cause seizures or stroke. A low-grade fever is common during crises.
Diagnosis — high-performance liquid chromatography is preferred and is on some states' newborn screening panels, so patients born elsewhere may not be diagnosed until later in life. Also the Sickledex screen, hemoglobin electrophoresis, solubility testing, and peripheral smear. Trait is usually 35–45% HbS and disease 95–98%, and the percentage does not change during crises.
The other labs — hematocrit typically 17–29% and hemoglobin 5–9 g/dL from shortened cell life, falling further in crisis or aplastic crisis. The reticulocyte count is high, indicating long-standing anemia; total bilirubin is high from released iron and bilirubin; and the WBC count is usually high from chronic inflammation caused by hypoxia and ischemia, not necessarily infection.
Imaging — skull x-ray changes from bone surface destruction and new growth, joint x-rays showing necrosis, and ultrasound, CT, PET, or MRI showing soft tissue and organ change. ECG changes indicate cardiac infarcts; echocardiography may show cardiomyopathy and a low ejection fraction.
The two priority problems — pain from poor perfusion and reduced gas exchange, and potential for infection, sepsis, MODS, and death.
The pain question that matters — ask whether this pain is typical of past episodes. If it is not, other causes or complications must be explored.
The bias problem, named plainly — many patients have had negative interactions with clinicians who suspect drug seeking, and concern about misuse leads to inadequate treatment. In fact opioid use in SCD has not risen in proportion to the opioid epidemic, and deaths are highly related to physiologic complications rather than misuse. Clinicians must be aware of their own attitudes — and a patient with opioid addiction who is in crisis still needs opioid medication.
Analgesia — start with IV morphine or hydromorphone on a routine schedule or by PCA, tapering to oral once relief is obtained. Avoid PRN schedules — they do not provide adequate relief. Moderate pain may be managed with oral opioids or NSAIDs. Target a level acceptable to the patient, such as 3 or less out of 10, that allows participation in self-care.
Crisis care bundle — remove constrictive clothing; encourage extension of the extremities to promote venous return; do not raise the knee position of the bed; keep the head of the bed no more than 30 degrees; give oxygen and analgesia; infuse normal saline; be ready for transfusion; keep the room comfortably warm, not cool; and check circulation every hour — pulse oximetry of fingers and toes, peripheral pulses, capillary refill.
Hydroxyurea — stimulates production of fetal hemoglobin (HbF), whose gamma chains bind oxygen more tightly than beta chains even at low tissue oxygen levels, so gas exchange improves and sickling falls. It works in some but not all patients and is a mainstay that reduces vasoocclusive events. It suppresses marrow including immunity, so regular CBCs are needed, and it raises the risk of leukemia and skin cancer with long-term use. It is a teratogen: a pregnancy test is done before starting, and strict contraception is required during treatment and for at least 1 month after stopping.
L-glutamine (Endari) — higher glutamine levels in red cells appear to lower oxidative stress, decreasing sickling and increasing cell lifespan, so mature erythrocytes rise and reticulocytes fall. Mixed with 8 ounces of liquid and taken orally; side effects include constipation, nausea, headache, abdominal pain, cough, extremity pain, back pain, and chest pain.
Crizanlizumab — a monoclonal antibody that binds platelets and inhibits selectin, the molecule that normally makes platelets, red cells, and endothelium adhere, reducing crisis frequency. Two 30-minute infusions at weeks 0 and 2, then monthly. Side effects: nausea, arthralgia, back pain, abdominal pain, fever.
Hydration and comfort measures — oral or IV fluids shorten the duration of pain episodes; fluids are usually infused until blood osmolarity normalizes. Add a warm room, distraction and relaxation, positioning with support for painful areas, aromatherapy, therapeutic touch, and warm soaks or compresses.
Infection prevention — high risk because of anoxic damage to the spleen. A febrile patient gets a sepsis workup: CBC with differential, blood cultures, reticulocyte count, urine culture, chest x-ray — and usually prophylactic oral penicillin, or erythromycin if allergic. Frequent thorough handwashing, anyone entering the room wears a mask, and strict aseptic technique for invasive procedures. Inspect the mouth every 8 hours for fungal or viral lesions, listen to the lungs every 8 hours, inspect urine for odor and cloudiness, and take vital signs at least every 4 hours. Urge pneumonia, annual influenza, and COVID-19 vaccination.
Oxygen detail — given during crises because lack of oxygen is the main cause of sickling, and it must be nebulized to prevent dehydration. If saturation is low, discuss ABGs and a chest x-ray with the provider.
Transfusion strategies — a simple transfusion dilutes HbS and raises HbA in more stable patients, but repeated transfusion causes iron overload. Red blood cell exchange (therapeutic erythrocytapheresis) removes whole blood, separates and discards the patient's red cells, and returns donor red cells with the patient's own white cells, plasma, and platelets — quickly lowering HbS without hyperviscosity or iron overload. The goal is removing more than 50% of sickle cells per procedure, and exchange is associated with lower subsequent stroke risk, making it the treatment of choice in acute stroke. Hematopoietic stem cell transplant can permanently correct the hemoglobin during childhood but is expensive and carries life-threatening complications.
Contraception counseling — barrier methods (cervical cap, diaphragm, condoms) are often recommended for sexually active women with SCD, especially those on hydroxyurea, as are etonogestrel implants. Combination hormonal contraceptives may increase clotting, especially in smokers, so progestin-only is recommended to reduce VTE risk — and any patient on hormonal contraception should not smoke. Pregnancy raises complication risk and crises can occur more often, so prenatal care and regular monitoring matter.
Prevention teaching — drink 3–4 L of liquid daily; practice deep breathing; wear socks, hat, gloves, and coat in cold weather; avoid alcohol, illicit substances, and all forms of tobacco and nicotine; avoid strenuous exercise, temperature extremes, overheating, and sun overexposure; avoid airplanes with unpressurized cabins and travel to high altitudes; when not in crisis do mild low-impact exercise three times a week; keep regular checkups; get influenza and COVID-19 immunizations yearly and ask about pneumonia vaccine; contact the provider at the first sign of illness; and tell every provider you have SCD, especially the anesthesia provider and radiologist.
Why
The most quietly devastating line in this chapter is that acute chest syndrome usually appears after admission for something else. The patient arrives in a pain crisis, gets opioids and fluids, and the crisis itself seeds fat emboli and marrow into the lungs — so a quarter of SCD deaths happen during the hospitalization meant to help. That is why respiratory assessment every shift is not routine monitoring in this population; it is surveillance for the thing most likely to kill them this week.

The anemias — cause by cause.

Anemia is a clinical indicator, not a disease — a reduction in red cell number, hemoglobin, or hematocrit occurring with many health problems. Under 14 g/dL in men and under 12 g/dL in women indicates anemia. Causes are dietary, genetic, marrow disease, or bleeding — and GI bleeding is a common reason in adults.
The shared cues — skin cool to the touch, intolerance of cool temperatures, pallor or ash-gray skin; tachycardia; decreased oxygen saturation and breathlessness on exertion; anorexia, diarrhea, glossitis, nausea, weight loss; difficulty walking; fatigue, numbness in hands and feet, reduced energy. Despite many causes, the symptoms and the nursing interventions are similar for all types.
Iron deficiency. anemia — the most common anemia worldwide, especially among premenopausal women, from blood loss, poor GI absorption, or inadequate diet. Any adult with iron deficiency should be evaluated for abnormal bleeding, especially GI. Healthy adults carry 3.5–4 g of iron. With chronic deficiency the cells are small (microcytic) and symptoms mild: weakness, pallor, fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, and fissures at the corners of the mouth. Serum ferritin under 10 ng/mL (normal 10–150 women, 12–300 men). Other causes: bariatric surgery, breastfeeding, menstruation, pregnancy or recent delivery, recent surgery or trauma.
Vitamin B&#8321;&#8322; deficiency (cobalamin deficiency) — conditions fail to activate the enzymes needed to move folic acid into precursor red cells, so those cells undergo improper DNA synthesis and grow larger — hence megaloblastic or macrocytic. anemia. Causes: vegan or protein-poor diets, small bowel resection, chronic diarrhea, diverticula, tapeworm, and overgrowth of intestinal bacteria.
Pernicious. anemia — failure to absorb B&#8321;&#8322; because of deficient intrinsic factor, normally secreted by gastric mucosa. It is an autoimmune disorder, so there may be a genetic predisposition and other affected family members, and it is more common in older adults with reduced gastric absorption. Causes of missing intrinsic factor include autoimmune disease, gastrectomy, gastritis, malabsorption syndromes, and surgery removing the end of the small intestine.
B&#8321;&#8322; presentation — mild or severe, usually developing slowly: pallor and jaundice, glossitis (a smooth, beefy-red tongue), fatigue, weight loss, plus numbness in the feet and hands and poor balance, because B&#8321;&#8322; maintains nerve function. In older adults the symptoms are vague enough that the disorder is easily overlooked.
Folic acid deficiency — a similar picture that also develops slowly, but paresthesias do not occur, because folate deficiency alone does not affect nerve function. Most common cause is poor nutrition — a diet lacking green leafy vegetables, liver, yeast, citrus fruits, dried beans, and nuts; second is malabsorption syndromes such as Crohn disease; and drugs contribute: methotrexate, phenytoin, sulfasalazine, trimethoprim, anticonvulsants, and oral contraceptives, plus chronic alcohol misuse, celiac disease, gastric bypass, and short bowel syndrome.
Aplastic anemia — impaired cellular regulation of the marrow, which fails to produce red cells after injury to the immature precursor cell. It usually occurs with leukopenia and thrombocytopenia together — pancytopenia — and onset may be slow or rapid. Causes: long-term exposure to toxic agents, drugs, ionizing radiation, or infection, sometimes following a viral infection, and often unknown. The most common hereditary form is Fanconi anemia.
Aplastic anemia workup and treatment — severe anemia symptoms (fatigue, weakness, dizziness, light-headedness, no energy); CBC shows severe macrocytic anemia, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia with an impaired reticulocyte count; marrow biopsy may show red cell-forming marrow replaced with fat. Infection is common. Treatment: transfusions for temporary relief, immunosuppressant therapy, and marrow stimulants (filgrastim, pegfilgrastim, epoetin alfa, eltrombopag), with stem cell transplant often curative.
G6PD deficiency anemia — an X-linked recessive enzyme deficiency expressed mostly in men, occasionally in women, often affecting African American men and men from Asia and the Mediterranean. G6PD drives glucose metabolism reactions that make ATP, which red cells have no other way to produce, so deficient cells break more easily.
G6PD triggers — drugs including sulfonamides, aspirin, quinine derivatives, chloramphenicol, dapsone, high-dose vitamin C, and thiazide diuretics; benzene and other toxins; and certain foods — fava beans are a known trigger, though other beans are not. New red cells have some G6PD but it diminishes with cell age, so the patient has no symptoms until exposure or severe infection. Then acute red cell breakage lasts 7–12 days with anemia and jaundice, and the reaction is self-limiting because only older, low-G6PD cells are destroyed.
Acquired autoimmune hemolytic anemia — from an autoimmune response; from drugs including methyldopa, penicillin, quinine, and sulfonamides; or alongside conditions such as sickle cell. anemia, thalassemia, and inherited enzyme deficiencies.
Why
Two anemias look identical on a blood count and diverge entirely at the bedside. B&#8321;&#8322; and folate deficiency both produce big, immature red cells — but only B&#8321;&#8322; maintains myelin, so only B&#8321;&#8322; deficiency numbs the hands and unsteadies the gait. That single discriminator is what makes the neurologic assessment, not the CBC, the test that decides which vitamin the patient needs.

Leukemia — treatment by type, and protecting the patient.

Why infection kills — the white cells are immature and nonfunctional or depleted, and sepsis can occur quickly. Infection arrives by autocontamination, where the patient's own normal flora overgrows and penetrates the internal environment, and by cross-contamination from another person. The most common sources are the skin, respiratory tract, and intestinal tract.
Skin as the last defense — the skin may be the patient's only intact defense. Keep it clean and dry, teach and provide daily hygiene, and turn immobile or fatigued patients every hour, avoiding bony prominences.
Pulmonary hygiene — every 2 to 4 hours. Listen for crackles, wheezes, and reduced breath sounds, and have the patient cough and deep breathe or use an incentive spirometer every hour while awake.
Prophylaxis — fluoroquinolone prophylaxis is usually used for high-risk neutropenic patients; research continues on prophylactic antibiotics in high-risk chemotherapy and allogeneic transplant patients.
AML — induction therapy: intense combination chemotherapy started at diagnosis to achieve rapid complete remission by killing as many cancer cells as possible. Usually cytarabine with daunorubicin or idarubicin, delivered over a 4- to 6-week hospitalization requiring frequent IV antibiotics and transfusions because the treatment itself induces dangerously low counts. Other effects: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, alopecia, stomatitis, and kidney, liver, and cardiac toxicity.
What counts as remission — all three must be true: no more than 5% of marrow cells are blasts, blood counts have returned to normal, and all signs and symptoms are gone.
AML — consolidation therapy: given after remission to reduce or eliminate residual leukemia cells, as intensive IV chemotherapy, oral maintenance chemotherapy, or stem cell transplantation, chosen by the patient's risk factors.
CML — tyrosine kinase inhibitors are the mainstay: imatinib, dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib, ponatinib, asciminib. These oral drugs are usually taken indefinitely. Teach avoidance of grapefruit and pomegranate and their juices, which interfere with the drugs, and note that TKIs are teratogenic — not for anyone pregnant or intending to become pregnant.
ALL — three phases: remission induction (combination chemotherapy, with imatinib mesylate for Philadelphia chromosome-positive disease, plus supportive care); CNS prophylaxis — cranial radiation plus intrathecal methotrexate, or high-dose systemic plus intrathecal methotrexate without cranial radiation, or intrathecal chemotherapy alone; and postremission (maintenance) therapy.
CLL — highly individualized. Asymptomatic patients may be managed by watchful waiting, with treatment decided by stage, symptoms, and disease activity. First-line options include acalabrutinib, ibrutinib, venetoclax with initial obinutuzumab, BR (bendamustine and rituximab), and FCR (fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, rituximab). Recurrent disease may repeat earlier agents or combine ibrutinib, venetoclax, or acalabrutinib with rituximab or obinutuzumab. Marrow or peripheral stem cell transplant is considered usually in patients under 66, and involved-field radiation may be used.
Myelodysplastic syndrome — drugs improve blood counts: azacitidine (IV or injection daily for 5–7 days then 21 treatment-free days, repeated two to six cycles); decitabine (IV every 8 hours for 3 days every 6 weeks, or once daily for 5 days every 4 weeks); lenalidomide (oral daily for 3 weeks then 1 week off, four or more cycles); luspatercept-aamt, an erythroid maturation agent given subcutaneously when anemia has not responded to an erythropoiesis-stimulating agent; and a combination of decitabine and cedazuridine orally for intermediate or high-risk disease.
Treating actual infection — antibacterial, antiviral, and antifungal agents are the main defense, chosen by the sensitivity of the causative organism and the severity of the infection.
Thrombocytopenia across the leukemias — AML and CML patients do not make enough platelets; ALL and CLL patients often have low counts too, and thrombocytopenia after allogeneic transplant is common and can be fatal if mismanaged.
The platelet numbers — normal 150,000–400,000/mm&sup3;, critical below 20,000. At the nadir (greatest marrow suppression) a transplant patient may fall below 10,000. Bleeding risk begins below 50,000, and spontaneous bleeding can occur below 20,000.
Bleeding precautions in practice — assess for oozing, enlarging bruises, petechiae, or purpura at least every 4 hours; inspect all stools, urine, drainage, and vomit for obvious blood and test for occult blood; measure blood loss as accurately as possible; and measure abdominal girth daily — an increase can indicate internal hemorrhage. Monitor the CBC daily. Platelets recover more slowly than white or red cells, so the patient remains at bleeding risk for weeks after discharge. A platelet transfusion may be needed, packed red cells for severe blood loss, and eltrombopag, an oral thrombopoietin receptor agonist, to increase platelet production.
Why fatigue is worse than the anemia alone explains — red cell production is limited in leukemia and MDS, and leukemic cells have high rates of metabolism, so they consume energy while the oxygen supply falls — with chemotherapy adding its own anemia on top.
Conserving energy — the practical bundle: reassure that fatigue is temporary but that returning to preillness energy may take as long as a year; teach that shortness of breath and palpitations are symptoms of overactivity, so stop ideally before they appear; space care activities at least an hour apart and schedule them when energy is higher, avoiding times right before or after meals; give a complete bed bath every other day, cleansing face, hands, axillae, and perineum in between; cancel or reschedule nonessential activities with the team; provide four to six small easy-to-eat meals instead of three large ones, high in protein and carbohydrate, with protein shakes or supplements; during extreme fatigue let others perform personal care, because independence in ADLs then can be detrimental; identify one or two preferred visitors allowed any time and limit others during rest; and monitor oxygen saturation and respiratory rate during activity to gauge tolerance.
Weighing activities against their cost — examine the schedule and judge each activity's usefulness: if the benefit is less than the fatigue it causes, work with the team to eliminate or postpone it — physical therapy and diagnostic tests not needed for current problems are candidates.
Home care planning — begins as soon as remission is achieved. Educate the patient, family, and caregivers about complications: risks of infection from central venous catheters, general infection risk, and thrombosis. Assess available support systems and coordinate home care accordingly.
Why
Notice how the two priorities in leukemia care pull against each other. Infection prevention argues for hygiene, turning, pulmonary toilet, and vigilance; fatigue management argues for bathing every other day, canceling activities, and letting someone else do personal care. The resolution is not a compromise but a ranking: the interventions that protect against sepsis are kept, and the ones that merely maintain independence are spent carefully, because in this patient exhaustion itself further impairs the immune system.

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, phase by phase.

What it is — stem cells taken from marrow or bloodstream. "Bone marrow transplant" is one specific type in which cells are taken only from marrow.
The logic of conditioning — high-dose chemotherapy, possibly with radiation, to eradicate the patient's own marrow and stop abnormal cell production. Total body irradiation is cytotoxic and immunosuppressive: it kills leukemic cells, makes room in the marrow, and weakens the immune system so it cannot reject the donor cells. These treatments are lethal to marrow — without the transplant the patient would die of infection or hemorrhage. Its two purposes are wiping out the marrow and myeloablation of cancer cells.
The five phases — stem cell obtainment, conditioning regimen, transplantation, engraftment, and posttransplantation recovery.
Types of transplant — autologous (self-donation, via peripheral apheresis or the patient's own stored cord blood); syngeneic (an HLA-identical twin or identical sibling); allogeneic (an HLA-matched relative, an unrelated HLA-matched donor, or a close tissue-type newborn donor via umbilical cord or placental blood). All three carry potentially life-threatening complications.
The blood-type misconception — patients often believe the donor's ABO type must match. Tissue type must be a close match; blood type is unrelated to tissue type and is not part of donor criteria.
Health equity in donation — tissue types vary among racial and ethnic groups, and diverse donors are not equally available. Encourage people of all races and ethnicities to register as donors.
Peripheral blood stem cell harvest — fewer stem cells circulate in blood than sit in marrow, but they can be mobilized to increase numbers, which reduces the collections needed. Usually one to five apheresis collections of 2–4 hours each.
Apheresis safety — the calcium problem: every apheresis needs an anticoagulant, usually heparin or citrate. Complications are catheter clotting and hypocalcemia caused by the anticoagulant. Verify ionized calcium before and after and monitor hourly or per orders. Citrate toxicity shows as numbness and tingling around the mouth, nausea, and vomiting; low ionized calcium as numbness or tingling in fingers and toes, abdominal or muscle cramping, or chest pain. Oral calcium may manage symptoms; a calcium infusion may be needed to correct. Monitor vital signs at least hourly — the patient may become hypotensive from fluid loss. Collected cells are then frozen and stored until conditioning is complete.
Bone marrow aspiration harvest — usually outpatient with local sedation, or IV conscious sedation for anxious patients. A hollow needle enters the iliac crest and marrow is aspirated, which is uncomfortable though it takes only minutes. Afterward: monitor for fluid loss and anesthesia complications, manage pain, keep the patient on their back 10–15 minutes with pressure at the needle site, and monitor breathing if sedated.
Harvest discharge teaching — have someone to drive them home; rest for 24 hours; tenderness at the site is normal; keep the bandage on and dry for 24 hours; showers after that when the bandage comes off, but no tub baths, pools, or hot tubs for at least a week. Report bleeding, drainage, swelling, increasing redness, fever, or increasing pain right away. Marrow naturally regrows within a few weeks; harvested marrow is filtered, treated to remove remaining cancer cells if autologous, and usually frozen.
Cord blood harvesting — umbilical cord blood has a high concentration of stem cells, drawn simply from the placenta after birth and before the placenta detaches, then processed and stored — for an unrelated recipient or in case the infant later needs them.
Phase 1 — conditioning, from admission to Day 0, ending with the stem cell infusion; some regimens include a rest day between chemotherapy or radiation and transplant. Nursing: IV fluids for hydration, antiemetics, ensure dietary trays are thoroughly cooked to reduce foodborne infection, and begin meticulous mouth care to minimize stomatitis — no alcohol-based mouthwashes, only a chemobrush or very soft toothbrush. Monitor the tunneled catheter and site closely.
Day T&minus;0 — the transplant: marrow, peripheral, or cord stem cells are thawed and infused through a central catheter over less than an hour to a few hours. Never use blood administration tubing — the cells get trapped in the filter and the patient receives fewer of them; use standard larger-bore IV tubing. Teach that common infusion effects include nausea, vomiting, cough, a garlic-like taste in the mouth, and pink to red urine, and to report any chills — monitor closely for fever or hypotension.
Phase 2 — transplant day to engraftment, usually Day +10 to Day +30: red cell, white cell, and platelet counts are low and infection risk is high. Maintain strict asepsis, teach that fatigue is a normal response, and anticipate transfusions.
Engraftment — the successful residency and growth of transplanted cells, averaging 14 to 21 days, sometimes aided by growth factors. Counts begin to rise. Engraftment syndrome may appear: noninfectious fever, skin rash, diarrhea, and weight gain, managed with corticosteroids and other immunosuppressives.
Chimerism testing — the ratio of recipient to donor cells. Mixed means both are present; progressive (rising donor percentage) indicates engraftment; regressive (rising patient percentage) indicates graft failure. Successful engraftment means only donor cells are present.
Graft failure — donated cells fail to grow and function. It happens more often with allogeneic than autologous transplant, with causes including HLA-mismatched grafts, the underlying disease, and a low stem cell dose. It is discussed with patient and donor in advance because if the cells fail to engraft, the patient dies unless another transplant succeeds.
Graft-versus-host disease — mostly in allogeneic transplants, when donor cells recognize the recipient's cells, tissues, and organs as foreign and attack them. Recipients get prophylactic medication. Acute GVHD occurs within the first 100 days and usually affects skin, GI tract, or liver: a burning red rash often on palms or soles, trunk, and extremities, plus nausea, vomiting, cramping, anorexia, diarrhea, and jaundice — treated with prednisone, methylprednisolone, or dexamethasone.
Chronic GVHD — one of the leading causes of complications and death after allogeneic transplant. Systemic effects across one or more organ systems: stomatitis, rash, hair loss, weight loss, shortness of breath, increasing abdominal girth, muscle cramps, joint stiffness, vaginal dryness or pain, penile irritation. If steroids fail, ibrutinib, belumosudil, or ruxolitinib or other immunosuppressive combinations are used. Alert the oncologist about any of these symptoms right away — early intervention prevents progression.
Sinusoidal obstructive syndrome (venoocclusive disease) — days to weeks after transplant, when sinusoidal endothelial cells are damaged, inflammation is magnified, and coagulation and fibrinolytic pathways activate, damaging the liver. Higher risk with preexisting liver or lung disease, older age, and an allogeneic graft. Symptoms: weight gain, edema, jaundice, painful hepatomegaly, ascites, thrombocytopenia, sometimes shortness of breath and fluid overload.
Managing SOS — there is no way to open the liver vessels, so treatment is supportive and early detection improves survival. Fluid management is crucial: assess daily for weight gain, fluid retention, ascites, and hepatomegaly; keep strict intake and output; monitor renal and liver function and PT and PTT closely. Severe cases may receive IV defibrotide.
Phase 3 — engraftment to discharge: counts recover and healing begins. Continue strict asepsis through discharge; teach infection avoidance at home, which symptoms to report immediately, and adherence to follow-up. Moderate activity as directed — exhaustion causes fatigue, which further impairs the immune system. Teach family that the patient should not be cleaning the home during this phase.
Phase 4 — early convalescence, from discharge to a year or longer: counts continue recovering but infection risk stays high because the immune system is not fully functioning, so prophylactic antibiotics are given. Assess thoroughly at every appointment to catch infection at its earliest onset, and reinforce the antibiotic schedule. Helping the patient maintain hope through this long stretch matters; refer to mental health support as needed.
Phase 5 — late convalescence, one year onward: ideally the immune system is fully recovered, but late complications such as organ dysfunction or leukemia recurrence remain possible. Continue thorough assessment, encourage a gradual return to pretransplant activities, and collaborate with the oncologist to begin the recommended vaccination sequence.
Why
Conditioning is the part of this process that reorders your intuitions. The treatment deliberately destroys the patient's marrow — the therapy itself is lethal, and the transplant is the rescue. Everything in phases 2 through 4 follows from that: the strict asepsis, the cooked food, the soft toothbrush, the ban on the patient cleaning their own house, all exist because for weeks there is no immune system at all, and then for a year only a partial one.

The shared anemia picture.

Fatigue, pallor, dyspnea on exertion, tachycardia, dizziness, cold intolerance, headache — and in older adults confusion or a fall. Ask how fast it developed: how symptomatic a patient is depends on speed as much as on the hemoglobin value. Look for the specific markers — spoon nails and a smooth sore tongue (iron), a beefy red tongue with paresthesias, ataxia, and poor proprioception (B&#8321;&#8322;), jaundice, dark urine, and splenomegaly (hemolysis).

Sickle cell specifics.

Previous crises and their triggers; all activities and events in the past 24 hours; energy level 0 to 10. Assess for acute chest syndrome (fever, chest and limb pain, tachypnea, cough, wheezing, infiltrates) — it accounts for a quarter of deaths and usually develops after admission. Jaundice at the sclera closest to the cornea, palms, soles, and hard palate every 4 hours; leg ulcers; priapism — report immediately; and cognitive or behavioral change as the earliest sign of cerebral hypoxia.

Malignancy and DIC.

Leukemia and lymphoma: bone pain, fever, night sweats, weight loss, the B symptoms, painless node enlargement. Myeloma: bone pain with fractures, hypercalcemia, kidney failure, anemia. DIC: oozing from every puncture site and mucous membrane with simultaneous organ ischemia, low platelets and fibrinogen, prolonged PT and aPTT, high D-dimer.

D — Diagnose & prioritize.

1. Infection with potential for sepsis related to neutropenia or impaired immunity — infection is the leading cause of death in leukemia, and in febrile neutropenia mortality climbs with every hour antibiotics are delayed.
2. Potential for bleeding related to thrombocytopenia or consumption of clotting factors.
3. Acute pain related to vasoocclusive crisis, bone involvement, or mucositis — with the patient's report as the measure.
4. Decreased gas exchange and activity intolerance related to anemia and, in sickle cell disease, to vasoocclusion.
5. Impaired oral mucous membranes related to chemotherapy, radiation, or candidiasis.
6. Anxiety and anticipatory grieving related to diagnosis, treatment burden, and uncertain prognosis.
Why
Pain is ranked third and treated first in a sickle cell crisis, which is not a contradiction but a warning. These patients are chronically undertreated and wrongly labelled drug-seeking; opioid use in this population has not risen with the opioid epidemic, and a patient with opioid addiction who is in crisis still needs opioid medication.

P — Plan.

Cultures drawn and broad-spectrum antibiotics infusing within 1 hour of a neutropenic fever — cultures first, but never delaying the drug.
Remains free of new infection; temperature, MAP, urine output, and mentation stable with no progression to shock.
No bleeding event; platelets transfused per threshold; no neurologic change.
Pain at a level the patient names as acceptable, reached with scheduled rather than PRN analgesia, allowing participation in self-care.
In sickle cell disease: hydrated, warm, oxygenated, and free of acute chest syndrome; crisis triggers identified and a prevention plan stated.
Counts recover; the patient and family state the fever rule, protective precautions, bleeding precautions, and when to call rather than wait until morning.
Why
Naming the fever threshold inside the outcome rather than in the teaching notes is deliberate — 100.4&deg;F is not a number to mention once. It is the number that changes the plan, and the patient at home is the one who has to act on it.

Dependent.

Cultures from the line and a peripheral site, imaging, then antibiotics within the hour; antifungals and antivirals. Transfusions per threshold with full verification. Growth factors and eltrombopag. Chemotherapy by phase — induction, consolidation, maintenance — or TKIs for CML (avoid grapefruit and pomegranate; teratogenic). Hydroxyurea for sickle cell disease (suppresses marrow; teratogen requiring contraception during and 1 month after). Crisis care: oxygen nebulized, aggressive hydration, scheduled opioids, warmth, and prompt treatment of infection.

Collaborative.

Oncology and possibly critical care; pharmacy for antimicrobial dosing; dietitian; social work and palliative care; contraception counseling in sickle cell disease (progestin-only preferred; combination hormones raise clot risk, especially in smokers); and prevention teaching — 3–4 L of fluid daily, avoid triggers, yearly immunizations, contact the provider at the first sign of illness.

E — Evaluate.

Time-stamped first: cultures drawn and antibiotics started within the hour. Then temperature trending down, MAP and urine output adequate, mentation clear. No bleeding; platelet count responding; oral lesions improving. Counts recovering, with the reticulocyte count and the ANC as the objective evidence. Pain controlled on a schedule and the patient participating in care. Teach-back confirms the fever rule and the precautions.
Unmet looks like: persistent fever after 72 hours of antibiotics, falling pressure, rising lactate, confusion, oliguria, new bleeding, a platelet count still falling, or in sickle cell disease new chest pain, fever, and tachypnea — acute chest syndrome.
Why
One evaluation trap is specific to marrow recovery: as the ANC returns, the patient may look worse before better, because returning neutrophils finally generate the inflammation — the infiltrate on the film, the redness at the site — that was invisible while there were none. A new finding during count recovery is not always a new infection.
